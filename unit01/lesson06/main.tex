\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lilac, colframe=plum, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will read $f^{-1}$ as the function that
    \textit{gives back the question} --- if $f(a) = b$, then $f^{-1}(b) = a$ ---
    and derive the topic from that one sentence: build $f^{-1}$ from a table by
    trading rows and explain the domain/range swap and the reflection over
    $y = x$ as the same fact; decide whether a function is one-to-one and
    restrict a domain when it is not; find $f^{-1}(x)$ by swapping and solving;
    and verify a pair by composition in both directions.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topic 2.8 --- Skills 1.A, 2.B)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{1.A} --- Solve equations and inequalities represented analytically,
with and without technology. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item An answer that came from \textit{two} questions cannot be sent back.
        A function is invertible on a domain exactly when it is
        \textbf{one-to-one} there; otherwise questions must be thrown away ---
        a \textbf{domain restriction}. (2.8.A.1)
  \item The analytic method: write $y = f(x)$, swap $x$ and $y$, solve for $y$,
        rename $f^{-1}(x)$. Step 2 \textit{is} ``make the answer the new
        question''; the rest is algebra. (2.8.B.4)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{2.B} --- Construct equivalent graphical, numerical, analytical, and
verbal representations of functions. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item If $f(a) = b$ then $f^{-1}(b) = a$. The questions you may ask $f^{-1}$
        are exactly the answers $f$ gave, so the \textbf{domain and range
        swap}. (2.8.A.2)
  \item Questions live on the $x$-axis and answers on the $y$-axis, so trading
        them sends $(a,b)$ to $(b,a)$ --- the graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the
        reflection of $y = f(x)$ over $y = x$. Same fact as the table trade.
        (2.8.B.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{Synthesis} --- inverses are checked, not asserted. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Verification by composition: hand $f$'s answer back and the question
        returns, so $f(f^{-1}(x)) = f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$, the identity function
        Lesson 1.5 named. (2.8.B.1)
  \item \textbf{Notation warning:} $f^{-1}(x) \ne \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. The
        reciprocal does arithmetic \textit{to the answer}; the inverse goes back
        to the \textit{question}. Today's signature error.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.28\linewidth} X @{}}
Inverse function      & The function $f^{-1}$ that gives back the question: if $f(a) = b$, then $f^{-1}(b) = a$. Read ``$f$-inverse.'' \\
One-to-one            & No two different questions share an answer. A function has an inverse exactly when it is one-to-one. \\
Domain restriction    & Throwing questions away until the function is one-to-one on what is left, so an inverse exists there. \\
Identity function     & $I(x) = x$ --- hands back its own input. Composing a function with its inverse produces it, in either order. \\
Reflection over $y=x$ & Every point $(a,b)$ on $f$ becomes $(b,a)$ on $f^{-1}$; the graphs are mirror images across $y = x$. \\
$f^{-1}$ vs.\ $1/f$   & \TallMath{f^{-1}(x) \ne \frac{1}{f(x)}} --- one goes back to the question, the other is arithmetic on the answer. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: solving a linear equation for $x$ (Algebra 1), evaluating a
    composition and naming the handoff (1.5), and reading a table backwards
    (1.1). Every item is load-bearing. Item 1(a) solves $3x + 2 = 14$ --- the
    hook's receipt, answered before it has a name, and exactly Step 3 of Part 4.
    Item 2 has students compute $f(g(11)) = 11$ and $g(f(3)) = 3$ with no
    explanation offered; Part 5 reveals that the $g$ they were handed was
    $P^{-1}$. Item 3(c) finds two inputs sharing the output 3, which is the
    one-to-one failure Part 3 is built on.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{Two \$14 parking receipts.} A receipt records only what was paid ---
    the \textit{answer}. What you want to know is how long the car was parked
    --- the \textit{question}.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Garage A charges $P(x) = 3x + 2$ for $x$ hours. Garage B posts a
              price list where 2 hours and 3 hours both cost \$14.
        \item Ask for the parking time on each receipt. Garage A: four hours,
              and everyone can defend it. Garage B: the room stalls --- the
              receipt does not say.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} both receipts hold an answer. One of them gives
    the question back and the other does not. Today is about the machine that
    runs a function backwards --- and about when there is no such machine.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: Giving Back the Question} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Draw the two-arrow picture: $a$ on the left, $b$ on the right, a plum
        arrow across the top labeled $f$, a gold arrow back along the bottom
        labeled $f^{-1}$. Label the boxes \textit{the question} and
        \textit{the answer}. That picture is the lesson.
  \item The spine, repeated verbatim all period: \textbf{the inverse gives back
        the question.} If $f(a) = b$, then $f^{-1}(b) = a$.
  \item Deliver the notation warning \textit{now}, before any algebra, and make
        it numerical: $P^{-1}(14) = 4$, while $1/P(14) = 1/44$. The reciprocal
        does arithmetic to the \textit{answer}; the inverse goes back to the
        \textit{question}. Two different trips, two wildly different numbers.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: The Answers Become the Questions} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Fill the $P$ row, then build $P^{-1}$ by \textbf{trading rows}. Nothing
        is computed --- say so out loud, because half the room will want to
        recompute.
  \item The domain/range swap is then a consequence, not a rule: the questions
        you may ask $P^{-1}$ are exactly the answers $P$ gave (2.8.A.2).
  \item Same fact, drawn: questions on the $x$-axis, answers on the $y$-axis,
        so trading them sends $(a,b)$ to $(b,a)$ --- reflection over $y = x$
        (2.8.B.3). Students \textit{read} the figure; nobody draws.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 3: When the Answer Gives Back Nothing} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Return to Garage B while the hook is still warm. $B^{-1}(14)$ is 2 or 3
        --- and a function gets one answer per question. So $B$ has no inverse
        (2.8.A.1).
  \item Define one-to-one \textit{off that}, not before it. Then point back at
        warm-up item 3: $q(1) = q(3) = 3$.
  \item $f(x) = x^2$: the answer 4 came from $-2$ and $2$. Restrict to
        $x \ge 0$ and $f^{-1}(x) = \sqrt{x}$. Insist the domain rides in the
        sentence --- ``$x^2$ has no inverse'' is false.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 4: Swap and Solve} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Motivate before the steps: a table only gives back the questions
        already printed on it. Then the four steps, with Step 2 named as the
        inversion --- the old answer becomes the new question (2.8.B.4).
  \item Do the garage first: $P^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x-2}{3}$, then
        $P^{-1}(14) = 4$, closing the hook analytically and matching Part 2's
        table.
  \item Point at the warm-up: solving $3x + 2 = 14$ gave one question back;
        swapping and solving does it for every answer at once. That sentence is
        why the method exists.
  \item Then $f(x) = 4x - 1$ for repetition. Do not let the class talk you out
        of the second example.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 5: The Check --- Hand the Answer Back} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{This is where Lesson 1.5 pays off.} In $f(f^{-1}(x))$ the
        \textbf{handoff} is whatever $f^{-1}$ produced; if $f^{-1}$ gives back
        questions, the far end is where you started (2.8.B.1).
  \item Verify $P$ against $P^{-1}$ in both orders, line by line, then name
        $I(x) = x$ --- the identity function 1.5 promised would come back.
  \item Put the warm-up on the board: they ran this check on $P$ and $P^{-1}$
        before either had a name. Algebra just does every input at once.
  \item Insist on \textit{both} directions. One composition alone does not
        settle it.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on the guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} writing $f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. The
        lesson's signature error. Cold-call with numbers, never with a rule:
        ``$P(4) = 14$. What is $P^{-1}(14)$? Now what is $1/P(14)$? Same
        number?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} recomputing outputs in Part 2 instead of trading
        rows. Ask: ``What did you have to calculate?'' Nothing --- that is the
        point.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} skipping the swap in Step 2 and solving the
        original equation for $x$. Ask which variable is the input of the
        \textit{new} function.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} verifying only $f(f^{-1}(x))$ and stopping. Both
        directions, every time.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} ``$x^2$ has no inverse.'' It has none
        \textit{on all real numbers}; on $x \ge 0$ it has one. Push for the
        domain in the sentence.
  \item \textbf{Spine check:} when a student is stuck, ask ``which number is
        the answer here, and which is the question?'' If they cannot say, the
        arithmetic will not save them.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Charging Station} in leveled
tiers. Every tier uses the same instrument --- range $R(t) = 25t + 10$ from
charging time --- so groups can be re-formed mid-period with no new setup. The
context carries the spine physically: the dashboard reports only miles, and the
one thing the driver actually wants to know is the time.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Read the table forwards, then backwards,
        build $R^{-1}$ by trading rows, fill a units table that shows hours and
        miles trading places, and write one sentence on what $R^{-1}$ does.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Find $R^{-1}(x)$ by swapping and solving,
        check it against the table, answer ``how long to reach 160 miles?'',
        verify the pair in both directions, and then run the $1/R$ comparison in
        numbers --- 2 hours against $1/1510$.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} A battery temperature $T(t) = (t-3)^2 + 50$
        is not one-to-one on $0 \le t \le 6$; find two times at $54^\circ$,
        restrict, invert, and test. Then build cost-from-miles as
        $C(R^{-1}(x))$ --- a chain only the inverse makes possible, whose
        handoff is a number of hours.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Given $f(6) = -2$, state $f^{-1}(-2)$ and write one sentence on why it
        is not $-\tfrac{1}{2}$.
  \item Find $f^{-1}(x)$ for $f(x) = 2x - 5$, then evaluate $f^{-1}(9)$ and
        check it forwards.
  \item From a table, say why $h$ has no inverse --- naming the repeated answer
        and the two questions it came from.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} item 1(b) is the diagnostic and the fastest scan on
the page; a student who writes anything reciprocal-flavored needs Part 1
retaught in tomorrow's warm-up window regardless of how items 2 and 3 went.
Item 2's forward check catches the student who inverted correctly but does not
believe it. Item 3 misses split two ways: naming 5 but not both of 1 and 3 is
incomplete reading, while naming 12 or 8 means the one-to-one idea did not land.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 6 problems --- table reversal plus a second table with no
inverse, two swap-and-solve inverses, a both-directions verification, the
delivery-fee context (including the $f^{-1}$ versus $1/f$ comparison in
numbers), domain restriction for $(x-2)^2$ with point-swapping and the mirror
line, and one AP-style multiple-choice item on what $f(3) = -2$ says about
$f^{-1}$.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} \textit{Shoes and socks.} For $f(x) = 2x$ and
$g(x) = x+5$, students invert the composite $(f \circ g)(x)$ and discover that
$g^{-1}(f^{-1}(x))$ matches while $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(x))$ does not --- undoing a
chain starts at the last step.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.7, Function Model Selection and Construction)
asks which kind of function fits a context --- and the question people actually
ask a finished model is the backwards one. Long-range: in Unit 5 the logarithm
arrives as nothing more than the inverse of the exponential function, built by
exactly today's method.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. All three items are planted. Item
1(a) is the hook's receipt --- $3x + 2 = 14$ gives 4 --- solved before anyone
calls it an inverse; when you reach Part 4 Step 3, say out loud that they
already did this once, for one answer, and that swapping and solving does it for
all of them. Item 2 is the one that pays off latest: students get
$f(g(11)) = 11$ and $g(f(3)) = 3$ with no explanation offered. \textbf{Do not
explain it at the warm-up} --- let it sit until Part 5, then put it back on the
board and say ``you verified a pair of inverses before I told you what one
was.'' Item 3(c) is the one-to-one failure in miniature, and Part 3 calls back
to it by name.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The whole lesson is one sentence --- \textbf{the inverse gives back the
question} --- and the notes are built so every part is a consequence of it
rather than a new rule. Say the sentence at the two-arrow picture and then keep
using the words \textit{question} and \textit{answer} all period; the payoff is
that the domain/range swap, the reflection over $y = x$, the one-to-one
condition, and the composition check stop being four things to memorize. Front-
load the notation warning in Part 1 and make it numerical --- ``4 versus
$1/44$'' beats any amount of ``the $-1$ isn't an exponent.'' In Part 2 the
sentence to protect is that \textit{nothing is computed} when the rows trade;
students who recompute have missed the idea even when the numbers come out
right. Part 3 is deliberately early, while the Garage B receipt is still warm
--- non-invertibility is half the lesson here, not a footnote. Part 4 is where
the time goes; do both examples. If time runs short, compress Part 3's $x^2$
discussion to ``$f(-2)$ and $f(2)$ are both 4'' and keep the figure read in
Part 2, since the reflection is assessed on the homework.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
The charging station is chosen because the instrument reports the wrong
direction on purpose: the dashboard shows miles, and every human question on
that lot is about time. Make each tier say the units out loud --- Tier R item 4
exists only for that, and ``miles in, hours out'' is the sentence you want back.
Tier A item 5 is the best thirty seconds of the activity: 2 hours against
$1/1510$, and the follow-up question ``what would $1/1510$ even be measuring?''
is worth a whole-class share. Tier E item 1 is the only place a non-linear
restriction gets practiced; if the class never reaches it, assign homework
problem 5 as the substitute. Tier E item 2 is the deepest idea on the page ---
cost-from-miles exists only because $R^{-1}$ does --- and part (d) is a direct
callback to 1.5's units argument for non-commutativity.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Sort by item 1(b) first --- it is the fastest
read on the page and the one that predicts Unit 5. Any answer built out of
$-\tfrac{1}{2}$ goes in the reteach pile no matter what else is right. On item 2
accept $\dfrac{x+5}{2}$ or $0.5x + 2.5$; both are correct, and the second often
signals a student who solved cleanly. The forward check ($f(7) = 9$) is there to
catch the student who got the formula and still does not trust it. On item 3,
naming 5 but only one of the two questions is a reading slip worth a sentence of
feedback; naming 8 or 12 is the misconception itself.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 4 is the storyline problem and the one to scan first --- part (d) is the
notation warning in numbers, and a wrong answer there predicts trouble in
Unit 5. Problem 1(c) is the only place non-invertibility is written in prose;
read it alongside 4(d). On problem 6, distractor A swaps the roles of question
and answer, C applies the reciprocal to the wrong number, and D applies it to
the right number --- each is worth thirty seconds of autopsy, and all three
should be answered with ``which one was the question?'' The extension is
genuinely hard for this track and is also the cleanest 1.5-plus-1.6 problem in
the lesson; assign it to students who finished Tier E rather than to everyone,
and open with it if anyone gets it.
\end{teachernote}
