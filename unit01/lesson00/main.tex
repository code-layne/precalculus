    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will identify the input and output
    quantities in a real-world relationship and say which depends on the other,
    explain in everyday language what makes a relationship a \textit{function}
    (each input produces exactly one output), distinguish the allowed
    many-to-one case from the disqualifying one-to-many case, and read a
    pre-drawn graph or a table to say where a quantity is increasing,
    decreasing, or constant.

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
Identify the input and output quantities in a contextual relationship
and decide which depends on which. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item The input (independent) quantity is the one we choose or track;
        the output (dependent) quantity responds to it.
  \item Ordered pairs list input first, output second.
  \item The ``you-control'' test settles most cases in one question.
\end{itemize} \\
Decide informally whether a rule is a function, and justify the decision. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A function sends each input to \textit{exactly one} output ---
        a dependable machine.
  \item Many-to-one is allowed; one-to-many is not. The rule constrains
        each \textit{input}, never the outputs.
  \item Reversing the roles can turn a function into a non-function.
\end{itemize} \\
Read qualitative behavior from a pre-drawn graph or a table. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A graph tells a story: rising, falling, and flat stretches describe
        how the output changes as the input moves.
  \item A flat stretch means the output holds steady --- not that
        nothing is happening.
  \item Students interpret pre-drawn graphs; no freehand sketching.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.24\linewidth} X @{}}
Quantity             & Something measurable that can change: time, height, cost, temperature. \\
Input                & The quantity we choose or track; listed first in an ordered pair. \\
Output               & The quantity that responds; listed second in an ordered pair. \\
Independent variable & Another name for the input quantity. \\
Dependent variable   & Another name for the output quantity --- it \textit{depends} on the input. \\
Function             & A rule that sends each input to exactly one output. (Informal today; notation in 1.1.) \\
Many-to-one          & Two or more inputs sharing one output. Allowed --- still a function. \\
One-to-many          & One input with two different outputs. Not allowed --- not a function. \\
Increasing / decreasing / constant & How an output behaves as the input grows: rising, falling, or holding steady. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{The trustworthy machine.} A vending machine and a claw machine both take
    your money. Press B4 on the vending machine and you get the same bag of chips
    \textit{every single time}; drop the claw and you get\ldots{} who knows.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Which machine can you \textit{trust}? Why?
        \item What would it mean for the vending machine to ``break the rules''?
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} mathematics reserves a special name for the
    trustworthy machine --- a \textit{function}: same input, same output, every time.
    Collect student examples of dependable and undependable ``machines'' from life.
    Do not define anything yet; the hook only needs to establish that
    \textit{dependability} is the property worth naming.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: Quantities That Change Together} (10 min)

Everything in this unit is about two quantities linked so that one responds to the
other:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Minutes of talk time and the phone bill; hours a candle burns and its
        height; time since you dropped a ball and its height.
  \item For each pair, ask: which one do we \textit{choose or track} (input), and
        which one \textit{responds} (output)?
  \item Name the synonyms now: input = independent variable, output = dependent
        variable. Ordered pairs list (input, output).
  \item Land the ``you-control'' test as the portable version students can use
        on any context they meet later.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: What Is a Function? (First Look)} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item The vending-machine idea, stated carefully: a rule is a \textbf{function}
        when each input produces \textit{exactly one} output.
  \item Examples and non-examples, tested aloud: student $\to$ ID number (yes);
        month $\to$ students born that month (no --- one month, many students);
        number $\to$ its double (yes); shoe size $\to$ a student who wears it
        (no --- ambiguous).
  \item Then the asymmetry, which is the hardest idea of the period: two inputs
        may share an output (both twins have the same birthday), but one input
        may never have two outputs. Say it twice, in both directions.
  \item Keep it verbal --- notation is deliberately absent today.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: A Graph Tells a Story} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Project the bike-ride graph from the guided notes (distance from home vs.\
        time): ride away, rest, ride back.
  \item Read it left to right as a story: rising segment = moving away, flat
        segment = resting, falling segment = returning.
  \item Ask where the rider is at $t = 5$ without any formula --- the graph
        \textit{is} the information.
  \item Vocabulary in action: increasing, decreasing, constant. No sketching ---
        students only read and interpret.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: A Table, and Where the Unit Goes} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item The candle table makes the same three moves as the graph. The point is
        that the representation changed but the reading did not.
  \item Its hour-2-to-3 flat stretch is the table's version of segment B ---
        connect the two explicitly.
  \item Close on notes section 5, the unit map: read it aloud, do not add to it.
        Students should leave knowing that 1.1 gives functions their notation and
        that the rest of the unit is about how quantities change together.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} swapping input and output (``the bill determines the
        minutes''). Cold-call: ``Which quantity do \textit{you} control?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} rejecting many-to-one rules as non-functions.
        Ask: ``Two students both wear size 9. Does student $\to$ shoe size stop
        being a function?'' (No --- each student still has one size.)
  \item \textbf{Common error:} stating the rule about outputs (``each output has
        one input''). Insist on the direction: the rule constrains each
        \textit{input}.
  \item \textbf{Graph reading:} students describing a flat segment as ``nothing
        happening.'' Push for precision: the input (time) is still moving; the
        output is constant.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call:} ``Give me a rule from your life that IS a function.
        Now break it so it isn't.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: Graph Stories} in leveled tiers.
Every tier reads pre-drawn or tabled data --- no tier sketches anything.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Match the labeled segments of the bathtub graph
        (fill, soak, drain) to their descriptions and read values off the graph.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} A phone-battery table across a day: label each
        interval increasing/decreasing, identify input and output, and explain why
        battery percent is a function of time.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} A runner's distance-from-start story: complete a
        table linking distance behavior to whether speed is zero or positive,
        resolve the puzzle ``two times share the same distance --- does that break
        the function rule?'', and then reverse the roles to find a genuine
        non-function.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the input and output quantities for an oven preheating.
  \item Decide whether ``each car $\to$ its license plate number'' is a function,
        with one sentence of reasoning.
  \item From a small table of plant heights, name the interval where the height is
        constant and the interval where it is increasing.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Item 2 is the heart of the lesson --- look for the phrase
``exactly one'' (or an equivalent) in the reasoning, not just a circled yes. Sort
tickets by item 2 and revisit the many-to-one vs.\ one-to-many distinction in the
1.1 warm-up if more than a handful miss it.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems covering input/output identification in two contexts,
three function-or-not judgments with reasons, a candle-height table read, a
behavior-from-a-context item, and one SOL-style multiple-choice item.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} Two different vending-machine buttons dispense the
same snack --- is the machine still a function? Students explain why many-to-one
does not break the rule.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.1, Function Fundamentals) functions get their
notation --- $f(x)$ --- along with domain, range, and evaluating; in 1.2 we study
how two quantities change \textit{in tandem}.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. The four items are pure Algebra 1
maintenance --- this is also your first diagnostic look at the class. A student
stuck on item 1 or 2 needs watching when evaluation returns with $f(x)$ notation
in 1.1.
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[11]
\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
Keep Part 2 verbal and fast --- the examples table does the work, and formal
notation is deliberately absent (it arrives in 1.1; do not front-load it today).
The many-to-one / one-to-many table underneath it is the block worth slowing for:
students who can only state the rule in one direction will misread the exit
ticket. The bike-ride and candle figures are pre-drawn; students annotate and fill
blanks only. Section 5 is the unit map and is read-only --- assign it aloud in the
last minute of notes and do not add to it. If time is short, the second practice
item folds into the activity's Tier A discussion.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
Mixed-readiness groups work well today since no tier needs algebra. Tier R groups
should articulate the segment story aloud before writing. Tier E's last two items
are the deepest idea in the lesson --- the runner's distance is a function of time,
but time is \textit{not} a function of distance. If a Tier E pair gets both, have
them present the pair during the closing minutes; it is the same asymmetry the exit
ticket tests.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only (``mistakes happen, blanks don't''). Grade item 2
with a one-second scan for ``exactly one'' reasoning --- that phrase predicts
readiness for the formal definition in 1.1.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 5 is the first SOL-style multiple-choice item of the year --- tell students
to expect one on most homeworks. The extension answer (many-to-one is fine) mirrors
the Tier E puzzle, so students who did Tier E can be pointed at it as review rather
than new work.
\end{teachernote}
