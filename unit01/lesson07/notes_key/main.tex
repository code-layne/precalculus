\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{vocabbox}
\termblanklong{First differences}
\ansline{The differences between consecutive output values of equally-spaced inputs: $\Delta y_i = y_{i+1} - y_i$.}

\termblanklong{Second differences}
\ansline{Differences of the first differences; constant for quadratic data.}

\termblanklong{Domain restriction}
\ansline{A limit on the input values required by mathematics, context, or practical meaning.}

\termblanklong{Piecewise-defined function}
\ansline{A function defined by different formulas over different intervals of its domain.}

\termblanklong{Model assumption}
\ansline{A simplifying claim about the real world that must hold for the model to be valid.}
\end{vocabbox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{hookbox}
``A scientist hands you a table of mystery data. How do you decide which function type to use?''

\medskip
Without graphing, you can inspect the \ans{differences} to detect the function's degree.
\end{hookbox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{notesbox}{Rate-of-Change Clues: The Difference-Table Method}
\textbf{Setup:} Inputs must be \ans{equally}-spaced. Compute differences of the output column.

\medskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & $y$ & $\Delta y$ (1st diff.) & $\Delta^2 y$ (2nd diff.) \\
\hline
0 & 1  & ---         & ---         \\
\hline
1 & 4  & \ans{3} & ---         \\
\hline
2 & 11 & \ans{7} & \ans{4} \\
\hline
3 & 22 & \ans{11} & \ans{4} \\
\hline
4 & 37 & \ans{15} & \ans{4} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\textbf{Decision tree:}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item 1st differences \textbf{constant} $\Rightarrow$ model is \ans{linear} (degree \ans{1})
  \item 2nd differences \textbf{constant} $\Rightarrow$ model is \ans{quadratic} (degree \ans{2})
  \item 3rd differences \textbf{constant} $\Rightarrow$ model is \ans{cubic} (degree \ans{3})
  \item $n$th differences constant $\Rightarrow$ model is degree-\ans{$n$} polynomial
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{Key idea:} Constant \textit{first} differences mean a \ans{constant} rate of change.
Constant \textit{second} differences mean the rate of change is itself \ans{linear (changing at a constant rate)}.

\begin{teachernote}
The example table above has constant 2nd differences of 4, so the data is quadratic.
Students should notice 1st differences are not constant, prompting them to compute 2nd differences.
\end{teachernote}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{notesbox}{Shape and Context Clues}
When you have a description but no table, use context:

\medskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{|p{5.2cm}|p{5.2cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Context clue} & \textbf{Best model type} \\
\hline
Roughly constant rate of change & \ans{linear} \\
\hline
Area or 2-D measurement & \ans{quadratic} \\
\hline
Volume or 3-D measurement & \ans{cubic} \\
\hline
Multiple local maxima/minima & \ans{higher-degree polynomial} \\
\hline
Behavior changes at a threshold & \ans{piecewise-defined} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\textbf{Special rule:} A degree-$n$ polynomial can model a data set with exactly
\ans{$n+1$} points (with distinct inputs).
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{notesbox}{Piecewise Models}
A piecewise-defined function uses \ans{different} formulas over \ans{different} intervals.

\medskip
\textbf{Example scenario:} A delivery driver travels at a constant speed for the first
20 minutes, stops for 5 minutes, then accelerates home. This situation requires
\ans{3} pieces because the \ans{behavior / rate of change} changes over different intervals.

\medskip
To describe a piecewise model, state:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item The \ans{formula (or function type)} for each piece.
  \item The type of function for each piece (e.g., \ans{linear}, \ans{constant}).
  \item The \ans{domain / interval} conditions (where each piece applies).
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{notesbox}{Stating Assumptions and Restrictions}
Every model embeds assumptions. Fill in the sentence frame:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
``This model assumes that \ans{the relationship between variables} remains \ans{consistent / proportional}
over \ans{the modeled interval}. The domain is restricted to \ans{values where the context is meaningful}
because \ans{negative or extreme inputs have no real-world interpretation}.''
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

\medskip
\textbf{Example:} A quadratic models the area $A$ of a rectangle with a fixed perimeter of
120 ft, where $\ell$ is one side length.

\medskip
Assumption: \ansline{The shape is a perfect rectangle and the perimeter is exactly 120 ft.}

Domain restriction: \ansline{$0 < \ell < 60$ (length must be positive and less than half the perimeter).}

Range restriction: \ansline{$0 < A \leq 900$ (area is positive; maximum at $\ell = 30$).}
\end{notesbox}
