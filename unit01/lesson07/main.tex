    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will choose a function type for a data set
    or a context --- by reading first and second differences from a table and by
    reading shape clues from the description --- will \textbf{build} the model from a
    contextual restriction, and will state the domain restriction and the
    assumption the model rests on.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topics 1.13 \& 1.14 --- Skills 2.A, 1.C)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{2.A} --- Identify information from graphical, numerical, analytical, and
verbal representations to answer a question. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Over \textbf{equally spaced} inputs: constant first differences
        $\Rightarrow$ linear; constant second differences $\Rightarrow$ quadratic;
        constant $n$th differences $\Rightarrow$ degree-$n$ polynomial.
        (1.13.A.1--A.5)
  \item Context carries the clue when no table does: area $\Rightarrow$ quadratic,
        volume $\Rightarrow$ cubic, inverse proportion $\Rightarrow$ rational, a
        rule that changes at a threshold $\Rightarrow$ piecewise. (1.13.A.3, A.7)
  \item A degree-$n$ polynomial can be fit through $n+1$ points with distinct
        inputs. (1.13.A.6)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{1.C} --- Construct a new function from a context, a transformation, or a
regression. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Building a model from a restriction: write the constraint, solve it for
        one variable, substitute into the quantity you actually want.
        (1.14.A.1--A.2)
  \item A model is used, not just written: evaluate it, and compute the average
        rate of change $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ \textbf{with units} ---
        output units per input unit. (1.14.C.1)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{Synthesis} --- every model comes with fine print. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item The domain is restricted by the context, not by the algebra: negative
        widths and negative lengths are not modeling errors, they are
        \textbf{outside the domain}. (1.13.B.1--B.4)
  \item Naming the assumption is part of the answer. A model that fits the numbers
        can still be wrong about the world.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.28\linewidth} X @{}}
Function model        & A function built to stand in for a real situation, so the situation can be predicted and measured. \\
First differences     & The differences between consecutive outputs of \textbf{equally spaced} inputs. Constant first differences mean a linear model. \\
Second differences    & The differences of the first differences. Constant second differences mean a quadratic model --- the rate of change is itself changing at a constant rate. \\
Constraint            & The equation the context forces on the variables, such as ``the fencing totals 80 feet.'' It is what lets two variables collapse into one. \\
Domain restriction    & The inputs the \textit{context} allows, which is usually narrower than what the algebra allows. \\
Piecewise-defined function & A function given by different rules on different intervals; used when behavior changes at a threshold. \\
Inverse proportion    & \TallMath{y = \frac{k}{x^n}} --- as one quantity grows the other shrinks. Modeled by a rational function; Unit 3 takes this up in full. \\
Model assumption      & The simplifying claim about the world that has to be true for the model to be trusted. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: computing differences down an output column and judging
    whether a rate of change is constant (Lessons 1.2--1.3); solving a literal
    equation such as $2w + \ell = 80$ for one variable (the algebra Part 3 runs
    on); and computing an average rate of change from a table with correct units
    (Lesson 1.3). Item 2 is the load-bearing one --- students solve for $\ell$ and
    then evaluate $w(80 - 2w)$ without being told what it is. Part 3 reveals it as
    the area of today's pen.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{Eighty feet of fence and a barn.} The garden club has exactly 80 feet
    of fencing for a rectangular pen. One whole side runs along the barn wall, so
    that side needs no fence at all.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Take a vote before any algebra: \textit{should the pen be a square?}
              Most classes say yes. Record the vote on the board --- it will be
              wrong, and that is the point.
        \item Then ask the question the lesson answers twice: what \textit{kind}
              of function turns a width into an area, and how would you know
              before you built it?
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} today we answer that question two ways --- from a
    table of measurements, and from the fencing constraint itself --- and the two
    answers had better agree.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: Reading the Model Off a Table} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item State the requirement first: differences only mean something when the
        inputs are \textbf{equally spaced}. Write it on the board and leave it
        there.
  \item Table A ($y = 5, 8, 11, 14, 17$): first differences all 3 $\Rightarrow$
        linear (1.13.A.1--A.2). Table B is the garden club's five trial layouts;
        first differences $250, 150, 50, -50$ are \textit{not} constant, but the
        second differences are all $-100$ $\Rightarrow$ quadratic (1.13.A.4).
  \item Say the meaning, not just the rule: constant first differences mean the
        \textit{amount} of change repeats; constant second differences mean the
        \textit{rate} of change changes at a steady rate. Extend the pattern
        aloud --- constant third differences would mean cubic (1.13.A.5).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: Reading the Model Off the Context} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Fill the clue table together. Area $\Rightarrow$ quadratic and volume
        $\Rightarrow$ cubic are the two to make automatic (1.13.A.3): they follow
        from multiplying two lengths or three.
  \item Inverse proportion $\Rightarrow$ rational is \textbf{exposure only}
        today --- name it, show $y = k/x$, and say Unit 3 is where it lives.
  \item A rule that changes at a threshold $\Rightarrow$ piecewise (1.13.A.7).
        Close with the counting fact: $n+1$ points with distinct inputs can be fit
        by a degree-$n$ polynomial (1.13.A.6) --- which is exactly why fitting the
        highest degree you can is a bad habit.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: Building the Model from the Constraint} (14 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item This is the heart of the lesson and where the time goes. Four steps, said
        out loud each time: name the variables, write the \textbf{constraint},
        solve the constraint for one variable, substitute into the quantity you
        want (1.14.A.1--A.2).
  \item $2w + \ell = 80 \Rightarrow \ell = 80 - 2w \Rightarrow
        A(w) = w(80 - 2w) = 80w - 2w^2$. Two lengths multiplied --- quadratic, as
        Part 2 predicted.
  \item Then the payoff: check $A(20) = 800$ against Table B. \textbf{The table
        and the constraint produced the same function.} Say that sentence.
  \item Restrict the domain from the context: $0 < w < 40$. Ask why 40, not
        ``because the graph stops.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: Using the Model} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Evaluate $A(10) = 600$ and $A(20) = 800$. Average rate of change on
        $[10,20]$ is $+20$; on $[20,30]$ it is $-20$ (1.14.C.1).
  \item \textbf{Units are not optional:} square feet per foot of width. Make every
        student say the full phrase once.
  \item Sign change from $+$ to $-$ means the maximum sits between. Vertex at
        $w = -b/(2a) = 20$, area $800$ ft$^2$, length $\ell = 40$ ft. Return to the
        opening vote: the best pen is $20 \times 40$, \textbf{not} a square.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 5: The Fine Print} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Domain $0 < w < 40$; range $0 < A \le 800$ (1.13.B.1--B.4).
  \item Assumptions: all 80 ft is used, the barn wall is at least 40 ft long, the
        pen is exactly rectangular, no gate gap. If the barn is only 30 ft long
        the rule changes for small $w$ --- \textbf{piecewise}, and a callback to
        Part 2.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} taking differences when the inputs are not equally
        spaced. This is the lesson's signature error and it is silent --- the
        numbers still subtract. Ask ``what is the gap between your inputs?''
        before you ask what the differences are.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} stopping at the first differences, seeing that they
        are not constant, and concluding ``not a polynomial.'' Prompt: ``take the
        differences again.''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} in Part 3, writing $A = w\ell$ and stopping.
        A model must be a function of \textbf{one} variable. Ask: ``what does the
        constraint let you replace?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} $2w + \ell = 80$ becoming $\ell = 80 - w$ or
        $\ell = 40 - 2w$. Catch it early --- every later answer inherits it.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} the domain given as ``all real numbers'' or
        ``$w > 0$'' with no upper end. Push for the sentence: at $w = 40$ there is
        no fencing left for the length.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} an average rate of change reported as a bare number.
        No units, no credit --- square feet per foot.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Courtyard Committee} in leveled
tiers. Every tier uses the same courtyard scenario --- 60 feet of edging along
the building wall --- so groups can be re-sorted mid-period without new setup.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Complete a difference table for the
        committee's trial layouts, name the model type from the second
        differences, and match four short scenarios to their model types.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Build $A(w) = 60w - 2w^2$ from the 60-foot
        constraint, state the domain, evaluate at two widths, and find the
        maximum.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Compute two average rates of change with units
        and interpret them; then a code rule forces $w \ge 18$, moving the maximum
        to the endpoint --- the restriction changes the answer. Closes with one
        inverse-proportion item as a bridge to Unit 3.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Complete a difference table and name the model type it points to.
  \item Build an area model from a 40-foot fencing constraint and state its
        domain (worked space provided).
  \item Name the model type for a stated context and write the assumption the
        model rests on.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Item 1 checks 1.13.A (selection from a table), item 2
checks 1.14.A (construction from a constraint), item 3 checks 1.13.B (the fine
print). Item 2 is the diagnostic --- a student who writes $A(w) = w(40 - w)$ has
forgotten that two widths are fenced and needs Part 3 retaught in tomorrow's
warm-up window.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 6 problems covering difference tables (including a cubic),
matching contexts to model types, building a model from a perimeter constraint,
the full pen storyline (evaluate, average rate of change with units, maximum),
an inverse-proportion model, and one AP-style multiple-choice item on what
constant second differences imply.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} The barn wall turns out to be only 30 feet long,
so widths below 25 feet need fencing on part of the fourth side too. Students
find the threshold, write the two rules, and explain why the model is now
piecewise.

\textbf{Preview:} Unit 1 ends here. Lesson 2.1 (Quadratic Functions Revisited)
opens Unit 2 with the function $A(w) = 80w - 2w^2$ still on the board --- vertex
form, the axis of symmetry, and why $-b/(2a)$ works instead of just that it does.
Every model built today was a polynomial; Unit 2 is where polynomials get
studied on their own terms.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, calculators fine. Item 1 is the only place all period
where students see a table whose inputs are \textit{not} equally spaced --- part
(c) is the trap, and it is worth ten seconds of ``look at the $x$ row first''
when you review. Item 2 does the Part 3 algebra with no context attached; do not
tell them what $w(80-2w)$ is. In Part 3, put the warm-up back on the board and
say ``you built today's model before you knew what it was for.'' Item 3 is
Lesson 1.3 with units, and units are what Part 4 grades on.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
Part 1's requirement --- equally spaced inputs --- must be said before the first
subtraction, not after the first wrong answer. Part 2 is deliberately fast; the
inverse-proportion row is a name-drop, and if a student asks what a rational
function is, ``a fraction with a variable underneath, and Unit 3 is about them''
is a complete answer today. Part 3 is where the period is won or lost: do all
four steps out loud, and do not let the class skip the substitution because
``it's obvious.'' The single most important line in the lesson is the check at
the end of Part 3 --- the constraint and the table produced the same function. If
time runs short, compress Part 5 to the domain and one assumption, and let the
homework carry the range and the piecewise wrinkle.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
All three tiers run on the same 60-foot courtyard, so a group that finishes
Tier R can move to Tier A without re-reading the scenario. Tier R's matching
question is worth more than its table --- students who can classify from context
are ready for Unit 2. Tier A's step 1 is the one to check as you circulate: if
the constraint is written $w + \ell = 60$, everything downstream is wrong and
quiet about it. Tier E's code rule ($w \ge 18$) is the most valuable idea in the
activity: the vertex is still at $w = 15$, but 15 is no longer in the domain, so
the maximum moves to the endpoint. If the class never reaches Tier E, assign
homework problem 4(c) as the substitute.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Sort by item 2 first. Accept
$A(w) = 40w - 2w^2$ or $A(w) = w(40 - 2w)$ --- both are correct and the factored
form often signals a student who understood the substitution rather than
memorizing a formula. A domain of $0 < w < 20$ is the target; $w > 0$ alone is
half credit and goes in the reteach pile. On item 3, any defensible assumption
earns the point --- the skill is that the student wrote one at all.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 4 is the storyline problem and the one to scan first --- part (b)'s units
predict who is ready for Unit 2's modeling work. Problem 5 is the only
inverse-proportion practice students get before Unit 3; expect the arithmetic on
$k = F d^2$ to be shakier than the concept, and grade the domain sentence, not
the decimal. On problem 6, distractor A is the student who never took the second
differences, C is the student who counted rows instead of differences, and D
confuses ``constant second differences'' with ``second differences of zero'' ---
each is worth thirty seconds tomorrow. The extension is genuinely hard for this
track; assign it to students who finished Tier E, not to everyone.
\end{teachernote}
