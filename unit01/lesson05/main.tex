    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will read every composition
    $f(g(x))$ through the one number that sits between the two functions ---
    the \textit{handoff}, which is the inner function's answer and the outer
    function's question at once; evaluate composites from a table and from
    formulas by naming that number first; build $(f \circ g)(x)$ by
    substituting $g(x)$ for every $x$ in $f(x)$; explain non-commutativity and
    the domain of a composite as facts about the handoff; and decompose a
    function --- recognizing every horizontal move from Lesson 1.4 as a
    composition.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topic 2.7 --- Skills 1.C, 2.B)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{1.C} --- Construct new functions, using compositions, that may be
useful in modeling contexts, criteria, or data. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$: the outputs of $g$ are used as the inputs
        of $f$. The number that changes hands --- the \textbf{handoff} --- is
        the whole mechanism. (2.7.A.1)
  \item Because the handoff must be an input $f$ accepts, the domain of the
        composite is restricted to those $x$ whose $g(x)$ lands in $f$'s
        domain. (2.7.A.1)
  \item A composite value can be found from a table, a graph, or an analytic
        expression --- always by naming the handoff first. (2.7.A.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{2.B} --- Construct equivalent representations of functions and
expressions. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Composition is \textbf{not} commutative: swapping the machines swaps
        the handoff, so $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$ are typically different.
        (2.7.A.3)
  \item The identity function $I(x) = x$ makes the handoff the number itself,
        so composing with it changes nothing. (2.7.A.4)
  \item Build $f(g(x))$ analytically by substituting $g(x)$ for \textit{every}
        $x$ in $f(x)$ --- the handoff is now an expression, not a number.
        (2.7.B.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{Synthesis} --- build a chain, then take one apart. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Composition links two quantities that no single formula connects; the
        handoff is the quantity in the middle, and it has its own units.
        (2.7.B.1)
  \item Decomposition is naming the handoff: what would you compute first?
        (2.7.C.1)
  \item An additive or multiplicative transformation of $f$ \textit{is} a
        composition with $g(x) = x+k$ or $g(x) = kx$ --- which is why
        Lesson 1.4's inside changes acted on inputs. (2.7.C.2--C.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Composition            & Running two functions back to back. Written $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ and read ``$f$ of $g$ of $x$.'' The circle is not multiplication. \\
Inner and outer function & In $f(g(x))$, the \textit{inner} function $g$ runs first; the \textit{outer} function $f$ runs on its result. The letter written last runs first. \\
Handoff value          & The number (or expression) the inner function passes over: its \textit{answer}, and immediately the outer function's \textit{question}. \\
Domain of a composite  & The inputs $x$ that $g$ accepts \textit{and} whose handoff $g(x)$ is an input $f$ accepts. A handoff the outer function refuses gives no value. \\
Identity function      & $I(x) = x$ --- the do-nothing function, whose handoff is the number itself. $f(I(x)) = I(f(x)) = f(x)$. \\
Decomposition          & Writing a single function $h$ as $f(g(x))$ for simpler pieces --- composition run backwards, done by naming the handoff. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: evaluating $f(x) = 3x-2$ at two numbers and at an
    \textit{expression} with the parentheses habit from 1.1; reading a table
    forwards and backwards (1.1); and the inside/outside sort from 1.4.
    Item 1(c) is load-bearing --- $f(x^2) = 3x^2-2$ is exactly the substitution
    Part 3 needs, done once before it has a name, and $f(5) = 13$ is the number
    Part 3 lands on when it checks a value two ways. Item 2(b) rehearses the
    backwards read Part 2 leans on when a handoff has to be looked up as an
    input.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{Two registers, one jacket.} A \$60 jacket, a \$10 coupon, 6\% sales
    tax. Register 1 scans the coupon first, then the tax; register 2 scans the
    tax first, then the coupon.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Take a vote before any computing: same total, or different? Most
              classes split, and most students argue ``same --- it's the same
              two things.''
        \item Then work both on the board, and \textbf{write the middle number
              on the board too}: register 1's screen reads \$50 before it
              charges \$53.00; register 2's screen reads \$63.60 before it
              charges \$53.60.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} the totals differ because the \textbf{middle
    numbers} differ. That number on the little screen is today's whole idea:
    it is what the first function answered, and it is what the second function
    gets asked. Name it the \textbf{handoff} and keep the word all period.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: The Handoff} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Draw the two-machine picture with the middle arrow in gold and label it
        twice: ``$g$'s answer'' above, ``$f$'s question'' below. That picture
        is the lesson.
  \item Give the notation once and insist on it: $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$,
        read ``$f$ of $g$ of $x$.'' The circle is not multiplication --- say
        what it is \textit{not} out loud, because $f(x)\cdot g(x)$ is a
        homework distractor.
  \item The spine, repeated verbatim all period: \textbf{one number, two jobs
        --- an answer first, then a question. Find it, and write it down.} In
        $f(g(x))$ the letter written last runs first.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: The Handoff from a Table} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item $f(g(1))$ out loud in two recorded steps: handoff $g(1) = 3$, then
        $f(3) = 2$. Do not let anyone skip the first blank.
  \item Then $g(f(1))$: handoff is now $4$, and the answer is $4$. Same
        functions, same input, different handoff --- name it, composition is
        not commutative (2.7.A.3).
  \item $f(g(2))$: the handoff is $7$, and $f$ has no $7$. No value. That is
        the domain of a composite, at exposure depth (2.7.A.1).
  \item The ``your turn'' table has a \textbf{handoff row}. Make them fill it
        all the way across before looking up one value of $f$ --- it is the
        same device as 1.4's ``handed to $f$'' row.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: Handing Over an Expression} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item One sentence of method: substitute $g(x)$ for \textbf{every} $x$ in
        $f(x)$ (2.7.B.2). Say ``every,'' and have them circle each $x$ first.
  \item The handoff is no longer a number, it is an expression --- that is the
        only thing that changed. Point back at the warm-up: the handoff was
        $x^2$.
  \item Work $f(g(x))$ then $g(f(x))$ for $f(x)=3x-2$, $g(x)=x^2+1$: $3x^2+1$
        against $9x^2-12x+5$. Not even the same shape.
  \item Check $f(g(2))$ two routes: handoff $5$, then $f(5) = 13$; or straight
        from $3x^2+1$. Same number (2.7.A.2), and $13$ is the warm-up's own
        answer.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: Two Registers, Two Handoffs} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Name the hook's functions: coupon $c(p) = p-10$, tax $t(x) = 1.06x$.
        Neither alone answers ``what do I pay?'' --- chaining them does
        (2.7.B.1).
  \item Both orders symbolically: $1.06p - 10.60$ against $1.06p - 10$. The
        \$0.60 gap is at every price, because taxing first taxes the ten
        dollars you never paid.
  \item Close the part on the spine: swap the machines, swap the handoff.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 5: Decomposition, Identity, and 1.4} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Decompose $h(x) = (5x-1)^3$ by asking ``what would your calculator do
        first?'' --- that finds the handoff, and the inner function is its
        rule. Verify by substituting back (2.7.C.1).
  \item Identity $I(x)=x$: the handoff is the number itself, so nothing
        happens (2.7.A.4). Flag it as tomorrow's target.
  \item \textbf{Deliver on 1.4's promise.} $f(x+3)$ is $f(h(x))$ with
        $h(x)=x+3$; $f(2x)$ is $f(h(x))$ with $h(x)=2x$. The handoff is what
        $f$ gets \textit{asked}, so an inside change can only rewrite the
        question (2.7.C.2--C.3).
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on the guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} no handoff written down. This is the one to
        chase hardest --- a student who does $f(g(1))$ in their head has no
        place to be wrong out loud and no place for you to catch it. Cold-call:
        ``What number goes into the second machine? Say it before you use it.''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} running the outer function first --- computing
        $f(1)$ when the problem says $f(g(1))$. Point at the parentheses:
        innermost first, like any arithmetic.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} reading $(f\circ g)(x)$ as $f(x)\cdot g(x)$.
        Ask for the handoff; there isn't one in a product, and that ends it.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} substituting into only the first $x$ of
        $f(x) = 3x-2$. Have them circle every $x$ before replacing anything.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} decomposing backwards --- calling $f(u) = 5u-1$
        the outer function of $(5x-1)^3$. Make them substitute back; they
        built $5x^3-1$ instead.
  \item \textbf{Spine check:} when a student says ``it's just backwards,'' stop
        and ask for the handoff by name. The slogan is what fails them in
        Part 5 and again in 1.6.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Summit Cable Car} in leveled
tiers. Every tier uses the same two instruments --- elevation $E(t)$ from time,
temperature $T(e)$ from elevation --- so groups can be re-formed mid-period
with no new setup. The context carries the spine physically: the handoff is an
\textit{elevation}, a real quantity with its own units, sitting between minutes
and degrees.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Read $E(4)$ and $T(800)$ off two separate
        tables, discover they were one question, then fill a handoff row across
        the whole ride and name the handoff's units.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Build $T(E(t)) = 62-t$ from the formulas,
        interpret it (one degree per minute), solve for the moment it hits
        $55^\circ$, write why neither instrument alone answers the question
        (2.7.B.1), and then reverse the order --- $E(T(t))$ hands $E$ a
        temperature, which it does not accept.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Chain a third function (kiosk drink sales
        from temperature) and name both handoffs; rewrite a three-minute late
        departure as $E(t-3)$, a composition, and connect it to 1.4; then find
        $m$ with $m(T(E(t))) = t$ and meet the identity function a day early.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For $f(x) = 2x$ and $g(x) = x-6$, evaluate $f(g(10))$ and $g(f(10))$,
        recording the handoff for each.
  \item For the same $f$ and $g$, write $(f\circ g)(x)$ and $(g\circ f)(x)$
        simplified, then say whether they are the same function.
  \item Decompose $h(x) = (x+7)^4$, and name the handoff used to compute
        $h(3)$.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} item 1 is the diagnostic, and the \textit{handoff}
blanks are the part to read --- two matching totals means the student ran the
same machine twice and Part 2 needs a reteach in tomorrow's warm-up window; a
correct total with an empty handoff blank means the habit did not land even
though the arithmetic did. Item 2 misses split two ways: $2x-12$ in both slots
is a substitution error, while $2(x-6)$ left unsimplified is only bookkeeping.
Item 3(b) is the cheapest check that ``handoff'' means something to them ---
$10$, not $7$ and not $10^4$.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems --- table-based composition in both orders with
an explicit handoff row and one composite that has no value, analytic
construction of both composites for a quadratic and a linear function, the
register context at a new price, decomposition (including rewriting a
Lesson 1.4 horizontal shift as a composition), and one AP-style multiple-choice
item on $f\circ g$.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} Verify that $I(x)=x$ leaves $f(x) = 5x-2$
unchanged in either order, then hunt for a $g$ with $f(g(x)) = x$. Students who
find $g(x) = \dfrac{x+2}{5}$ have invented the inverse function a day early.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.6, Inverse Functions) answers the question the
extension raises: which function \textit{undoes} $f$? The test is a
composition --- $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$, the identity function from Part 5.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1(c) is the payoff:
substituting $x^2$ into $f(x) = 3x-2$ is a routine algebra question here and
the entire mechanic of Part 3 --- when you get there, say out loud that they
already did it, and that the thing they substituted was the handoff. Item 1(a)
is not filler either: $f(5) = 13$ is the exact number Part 3 lands on when it
checks $f(g(2))$ two ways, and students who recognize it get the ``two routes,
one number'' point for free. Item 2(b) reads the table backwards, which is the
habit Part 2 needs when a handoff has to be found as an \textit{input}. Item 3
is a one-line callback to 1.4 so Part 5's punchline has something to land on.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The single most valuable thing you can enforce today is \textbf{writing the
handoff down}, and the notes are built to make that unavoidable: two-step
layouts in Part 2, a dedicated handoff row in the ``your turn'' table, handoff
blanks in Parts 3 and 4, and a handoff column in the Lesson 1.4 table at the
end. Do not let a student skip the first blank because they can do it in their
head --- the habit, not the arithmetic, is what carries into 1.6 and into
Unit 4. Take the hook vote before computing, and put both middle numbers on the
board next to both totals; the \$50 and the \$63.60 are the reason the totals
differ, and if you only write the totals the class hears ``order matters''
as an unexplained rule. Domain of a composite stays at exposure depth: the
table value $f(g(2))$ with no answer is enough, and no student needs interval
algebra today. If time runs short, compress Part 3's second work block
($g(f(x))$) rather than cutting Parts 4 or 5.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
The cable car is chosen so the handoff is a physical quantity with units: an
elevation in feet, sitting between minutes and degrees. Make every tier say
``feet'' out loud --- Tier R item 4 exists only for that. Tier R's items 1 and
2 are deliberately the same question asked twice, once as two lookups and once
as a composition; let groups notice that themselves before you say it. Tier A
item 4 is the best written answer of the day (2.7.B.1) and worth a whole-class
share: nothing on that mountain measures degrees against time. Tier A item 5 is
the one to protect if a group is running out of time --- a reversed order whose
handoff has the \textit{wrong units} is a sharper argument for
non-commutativity than any pair of unequal answers. Tier E item 3 is the
on-ramp to 1.6; if any group gets there, open tomorrow with their $m(y)=62-y$.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Sort by item 1: handoffs $4$ and $20$,
totals $8$ and $14$. Two matching totals is your reteach pile. Read the
\textit{handoff} blanks before the totals --- a right total with a blank
handoff is a different diagnosis (habit not formed) from a wrong total (order
not understood), and only the second needs Part 2 retaught. On item 2 accept
$2(x-6)$ as well as $2x-12$; that slot is testing substitution, not
distribution. Item 3(a) has more than one defensible split if a student divides
it differently --- the test is whether substituting back rebuilds $h$, so read
their check rather than their letters. Item 3(b) has one answer, $10$, and it
is the fastest scan on the page.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 1(c) --- why $f(g(5))$ has no value --- is the only place the domain of
a composite is written in prose, and it is the item to scan first alongside
4(c). Problem 4(c) is what closes the loop with Lesson 1.4; a student who
cannot rewrite $f(x+6)$ as a composition did not hear Part 5, and the fix is
thirty seconds at the board with the handoff column from the notes. On problem
5, distractor A is $(g\circ f)(x)$, C is $(2x-1)^2$ squared term-by-term, and D
is the ``circle means multiply'' error --- each is worth thirty seconds of
autopsy, and D in particular should be answered with ``where is the handoff?''
The extension is the direct on-ramp to 1.6; if the class never reached
Tier E, assign it explicitly so someone arrives tomorrow having already built
an inverse.
\end{teachernote}
