\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{1. The Handoff}
A \textbf{composition} runs two functions back to back. We write it

\begin{center}
$(f \circ g)(x) = f\big(g(x)\big)$, \quad read ``$f$ of $g$ of $x$.''
\end{center}

The circle is \textit{not} multiplication. Here is the whole idea in one
picture.

\smallskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm, y=1cm]
  \draw[thick, plum, fill=lilac, rounded corners=2pt]
    (-0.8,-0.5) rectangle (0.8,0.5);
  \node at (0,0) {\large $g$};
  \draw[thick, plum, fill=lilac, rounded corners=2pt]
    (4.2,-0.5) rectangle (5.8,0.5);
  \node at (5,0) {\large $f$};
  \draw[->, very thick, plum] (-2.4,0) -- (-0.9,0);
  \node[above, font=\small] at (-1.65,0.08) {$x$};
  \draw[->, very thick, goldacc] (0.9,0) -- (4.1,0);
  \node[above, font=\small] at (2.5,0.10) {$g(x)$};
  \draw[->, very thick, plum] (5.9,0) -- (7.9,0);
  \node[above, font=\small] at (6.9,0.08) {$f(g(x))$};
  \node[above, font=\small\itshape, text=slate] at (0,0.58) {runs first};
  \node[above, font=\small\itshape, text=slate] at (5,0.58) {runs second};
  \node[below, font=\scriptsize\bfseries, goldacc] at (2.5,-0.10) {THE HANDOFF};
  \node[below, font=\scriptsize, text=slate, align=center]
    at (2.5,-0.42) {$g$'s answer \textit{is} $f$'s question};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\smallskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lilac, colframe=plum, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2mm,
    left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, width=0.94\linewidth]
\textbf{The handoff.} In $f(g(x))$, the number $g$ hands back is the very
number $f$ gets asked about. One number doing two jobs --- an \textbf{answer}
first, then a \textbf{question}. Find it first, and \textbf{write it down}.
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Notice which letter runs first. In $f(g(x))$ the function written
\textit{last} is the one that goes \textit{first} --- exactly like innermost
parentheses in arithmetic.

\medskip
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{composite} & \textbf{runs first} & \textbf{runs second} \\
\hline
$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ & \blank{2cm} & \blank{2cm} \\
\hline
$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$ & \blank{2cm} & \blank{2cm} \\
\hline
$f(f(x))$ & \blank{2cm} & \blank{2cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
And one thing $(f \circ g)(x)$ does \textit{not} mean: \blank{3.4cm}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[22]
\begin{notesbox}{2. Finding the Handoff in a Table}
A composition never needs a formula --- it needs two lookups. So a table is
enough.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
\hline
$f(x)$ & 3 & 4 & 0 & 2 & 1 \\
\hline
$g(x)$ & 2 & 3 & 7 & 0 & 4 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip
\textbf{Example 1.} Find $f(g(1))$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, itemsep=3pt, leftmargin=4.4em]
  \item The inner function runs first. The handoff is $g(1) = $ \blank{1.4cm}
  \item Hand it over: $f($ \blank{1.2cm} $) = $ \blank{1.4cm}
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\textbf{Example 2.} Now swap the machines: find $g(f(1))$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, itemsep=3pt, leftmargin=4.4em]
  \item Now $f$ is inside, so the handoff is $f(1) = $ \blank{1.4cm}
  \item Hand it over: $g($ \blank{1.2cm} $) = $ \blank{1.4cm}
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
Same two functions, same starting input, a different \blank{2.2cm} --- and so
a different answer. Composition is \textbf{not commutative}: in general
$f(g(x))$ \blank{1cm} $g(f(x))$.

\medskip
\textbf{Example 3 (a warning).} Try $f(g(2))$. The handoff is $g(2) = $
\blank{1.2cm}. Now look for $f($ \blank{1.2cm} $)$ in the table --- it is
\blank{2.8cm}. A handoff only works if it is a question the \textbf{outer}
function accepts, and that restriction is the \textbf{domain of a composite}.

\medskip
\textbf{Your turn.} Fill the handoff row all the way across \textit{before}
you look up a single value of $f$.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & 0 & 1 & 3 & 4 \\
\hline
handoff: $g(x)$ & \blank{1.1cm} & \blank{1.1cm} & \blank{1.1cm}
  & \blank{1.1cm} \\
\hline
$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ & \blank{1.1cm} & \blank{1.1cm} & \blank{1.1cm}
  & \blank{1.1cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[20]
\begin{notesbox}{3. Handing Over a Whole Expression}
With formulas instead of a table, the handoff is no longer a number --- it is
an \textbf{expression}. Nothing else changes.

\begin{center}
To build $f(g(x))$, substitute $g(x)$ for \textbf{every} $x$ in $f(x)$.
\end{center}

You already did this on the warm-up: with $f(x) = 3x - 2$, the handoff $x^2$
turned $f(x^2)$ into $3x^2 - 2$. The only new part is the name.

\medskip
\textbf{Example 4.} Let $f(x) = 3x - 2$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 1$. Build both
composites.

\begin{work}
  f(g(x)) &= f(x^2 + 1) \\
          &= 3(x^2 + 1) - 2 \\
          &= 3x^2 + 3 - 2 \\
          &= 3x^2 + 1 \\
  g(f(x)) &= g(3x - 2) \\
          &= (3x - 2)^2 + 1 \\
          &= 9x^2 - 12x + 4 + 1 \\
          &= 9x^2 - 12x + 5
\end{work}

$(f \circ g)(x) = $ \blank{3.2cm} \qquad $(g \circ f)(x) = $ \blank{4.4cm}

\smallskip
These are not even the same \textit{shape} of expression, which settles the
question: order \blank{2.4cm}.

\medskip
\textbf{Two routes, one number.} Check $f(g(2))$ both ways. Step by step: the
handoff is $g(2) = $ \blank{1.2cm}, so $f($ \blank{1.2cm} $) = $
\blank{1.2cm}. Straight from the formula: $3(2)^2 + 1 = $ \blank{1.2cm}. A
composite value can come from a table, a graph, or an expression --- the
number does not care which route you take.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Two Registers, Two Handoffs}
Back to the jacket. Two functions act on the price $p$:

\begin{center}
\textbf{Coupon:} $c(p) = p - 10$ \qquad\qquad
\textbf{Tax:} $t(x) = 1.06x$
\end{center}

Neither one alone answers ``what do I pay?'' --- chaining them does. That is
what composition is \textit{for}: linking two quantities that no single
formula connects.

\medskip
\textbf{Example 5.} The jacket costs \$60. Run both registers.

\begin{work}
  t(c(60)) &= t(50) \\
           &= 1.06(50) \\
           &= 53.00 \\
  c(t(60)) &= c(63.60) \\
           &= 63.60 - 10 \\
           &= 53.60
\end{work}

Register 1's screen showed \$\blank{1.4cm} and it charged \$\blank{1.6cm}.
Register 2's screen showed \$\blank{1.4cm} and it charged \$\blank{1.6cm}.

\medskip
\textbf{Why?} Build both rules symbolically and the gap stops being a
coincidence.

\begin{work}
  t(c(p)) &= 1.06(p - 10) \\
          &= 1.06p - 10.60 \\
  c(t(p)) &= 1.06p - 10
\end{work}

The two rules differ by \$\blank{1.4cm} at \textit{every} price, because
taxing first charges you 6\% of the \blank{2.4cm} you never actually paid.

\smallskip
Swap the machines and you swap the \blank{2.2cm} --- that is what ``order
matters'' really means.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{5. Decomposition, the Identity, and Lesson 1.4}
\textbf{Decomposition} is composition run backwards: given one function, name
the handoff, and the two pieces fall out.

\medskip
\textbf{Example 6.} Decompose $h(x) = (5x - 1)^3$. Ask: with a calculator,
what would you compute \textit{first}? That number is the handoff, so its rule
is the inner function.

\smallskip
Inner: $g(x) = $ \blank{2.4cm} \qquad Outer: $f(u) = $ \blank{2cm}

\smallskip
Check by substituting back: $f(g(x)) = $ \blank{3.4cm}

\medskip
\textbf{The identity function.} $I(x) = x$ hands back exactly what it is given,
so the handoff is the number itself and nothing happens:

\smallskip
$I(f(x)) = $ \blank{1.8cm} \qquad and \qquad $f(I(x)) = $ \blank{1.8cm}

\smallskip
Hold on to this one --- next lesson hunts for a partner function that turns $f$
into exactly $I$.

\medskip
\textbf{Lesson 1.4 was composition all along.} Every ``inside'' change you made
last lesson handed $f$ something other than $x$ --- which is to say, it built
a handoff.

\smallskip
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{0.96\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{0.22\linewidth} X c @{}}
\hline
From Lesson 1.4 & Written as a composition & \textbf{the handoff} \\
\hline
$g(x) = f(x+3)$ & $g(x) = f(h(x))$ \quad where $h(x) = $ \blank{2.2cm}
  & \blank{1.6cm} \\
\hline
$g(x) = f(2x)$  & $g(x) = f(h(x))$ \quad where $h(x) = $ \blank{2.2cm}
  & \blank{1.6cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Now the reversal has a reason instead of a slogan. The handoff is what $f$ gets
\textit{asked}, so an inside change can only rewrite the \blank{2.2cm} ---
never the answers $f$ hands back.
\end{notesbox}
