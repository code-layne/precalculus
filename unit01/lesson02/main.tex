\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{2.B} --- Construct equivalent graphical, numerical, analytical, or
verbal representations of a function. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A function's input and output values vary in tandem according to the
        function rule. (1.1.A.2)
  \item A graph displays every input--output pair at once, so trends ---
        rising, falling, bending --- become visible. (1.1.B.1)
  \item A verbal description of covarying quantities determines the shape of
        the graph. (1.1.B.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function using
mathematical language and notation. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Increasing: whenever $a < b$, $f(a) < f(b)$. Decreasing: whenever
        $a < b$, $f(a) > f(b)$. (1.1.A.3--4)
  \item Concave up: the rate of change is increasing. Concave down: the rate
        of change is decreasing. (1.1.B.3--4)
  \item A zero is an input whose output is 0; the graph meets the horizontal
        axis there. (1.1.B.5)
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.24\linewidth} X @{}}
Vary in tandem  & Two quantities change together: each input's change produces a change in the output, linked by the function rule. \\
Increasing      & On an interval, larger inputs give larger outputs: whenever $a < b$, $f(a) < f(b)$. The graph rises left to right. \\
Decreasing      & On an interval, larger inputs give smaller outputs: whenever $a < b$, $f(a) > f(b)$. The graph falls left to right. \\
Concave up      & The rate of change is increasing on the interval; the graph bends upward like a bowl. \\
Concave down    & The rate of change is decreasing on the interval; the graph bends downward like a dome. \\
Zero            & An input where the output equals 0; the graph crosses or touches the horizontal axis there. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: evaluating a function at a negative input (parentheses
    habit), reading a table in both directions --- find $p(2)$ vs.\ solve
    $p(x) = 8$ --- and stating the domain and range of a function given as a
    list of points. Item 2(c) plants today's seed: as the inputs climb, what

\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{The car and the stop sign.} A car pulls away from a stop sign.
    Two quantities start changing at once.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Its \textit{speed} climbs from 0 --- quickly at first, then
              leveling off near the speed limit.
        \item Its \textit{distance} from the sign grows --- slowly at first,
              then faster and faster.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} no drawing, just words --- describe how each
    quantity changes as time passes. Both are increasing, yet the two stories
    feel different. Today's lesson builds the vocabulary that tells them
    apart: it is the \textit{rate} of change that differs, and
    \textbf{concavity} is its name.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: Two Quantities in Tandem} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item A hose fills a tank: as time $t$ increases, volume $V$ responds. $V$
        is a function of $t$ --- each moment has exactly one volume (from
        1.0/1.1).
  \item Table: $t = 0, 2, 4, 6$ min gives $V = 0, 8, 16, 24$ gal. Read it in
        tandem: every 2 minutes, 8 more gallons --- steady gain.
  \item Name the roles: input/independent = time, output/dependent = volume;
        connect to 1.1's domain language ($0 \le t \le 6$ so far).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: Increasing and Decreasing} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Definition with teeth: increasing means whenever $a < b$,
        $f(a) < f(b)$ --- larger inputs give larger outputs. Decreasing flips
        the output inequality. Say it in words \textit{and} symbols.
  \item Project the pre-drawn graph from the notes (rise--fall--rise). Students
        name the intervals with inequalities on the \textit{inputs}:
        increasing for $0 < x < 3$ and $6 < x < 8$, decreasing for
        $3 < x < 6$.
  \item Hazard to front-load: intervals are named by inputs ($x$-values),
        never outputs. And decreasing $\ne$ negative --- a function can fall
        from 10 to 2 while every output stays positive.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: Concavity --- How the Change Changes} (14 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Refill the tank with the drain half-open: $t = 0, 2, 4, 6$ gives
        $V = 0, 14, 20, 24$. Still increasing --- but the gains are 14, 6, 4.
        The rate of change is shrinking: \textbf{concave down}.
  \item Contrast table (savings account): gains 20, 25, 30 --- rate of change
        growing: \textbf{concave up}.
  \item Anchor to the hook: distance from the stop sign was increasing
        \textit{and} concave up (gains growing); speed was increasing and
        concave down (gains shrinking).
  \item Show the two bends side by side (bowl vs.\ dome, pre-drawn in the
        notes). Key question to drill: ``increasing or decreasing?'' and
        ``concave up or down?'' are \textit{independent} questions --- all
        four combinations exist.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: Zeros} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item A zero is an input whose output is 0; the graph meets the horizontal
        axis there. From the pre-drawn graph in the notes: zeros at $x = 2$
        and $x = 6$.
  \item In context, a zero is an event: the drone lands, the tank is empty,
        the hiker reaches the river. Students translate a zero into a
        sentence, the same way $f(3) = 7$ became a sentence in 1.1.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} naming intervals by outputs --- ``increasing
        from 1 to 5'' while pointing at $y$-values. Push: ``Increasing on
        which \textit{inputs}?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} decreasing confused with negative. Cold-call:
        ``Can a function be decreasing while all its outputs are positive?''
        (Yes --- cooling cocoa: 90 to 40 degrees.)
  \item \textbf{Common error:} concavity welded to increase/decrease ---
        ``concave down means it's falling.'' Cold-call: ``Give me a function
        that is increasing \textit{and} concave down.'' (The half-open-drain
        tank.)
  \item \textbf{Vocabulary check:} students should justify concavity with
        rate-of-change language --- gains growing or shrinking --- not just
        ``it looks bendy.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Hiker's Day} in leveled
tiers. Every tier reads the same pre-drawn elevation graph and its companion
table --- no tier sketches anything.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Read outputs off the graph ($E(0)$, the
        peak), circle increasing or decreasing on two named intervals, and
        translate the zero at $t = 5$ into a sentence.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Name every increasing and decreasing
        interval with inequalities, use the table of climb gains (200, 130,
        70) to justify that the climb is concave down, and interpret the
        zero in context.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Reason about the descent's concavity, decide
        whether \textit{time} is a function of \textit{elevation} (spiral to
        1.1's many-to-one idea), and match a verbal description of a second
        hiker to the correct behavior list.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item From a table of an online game's player counts (gains 10, 8, 6),
        circle increasing or decreasing, then concave up or down, and defend
        the concavity call in one sentence.
  \item Complete the definition: concave up means the rate of change is
        \_\_\_\_.
  \item From a draining-pond table ending at 0, identify the zero and write
        what it means in context.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Item 1's sentence is the hinge --- credit requires
rate-of-change language (``the gains are shrinking''), not shape talk. A
student who circles \textit{decreasing} for item 1 has welded concave down to
falling; reteach with the half-open-drain tank in the 1.3 warm-up window if
more than a handful do.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems covering increasing/decreasing and concavity
from a verbal description (growing pumpkin), a full graph read (intervals,
zero, domain and range), a table-differences concavity call, a
concept question (decreasing and concave up at once --- possible?), and one
AP-style multiple-choice item on ``increasing and concave down.''

\textbf{Extension (optional):} A hiker who rests mid-climb --- students name
each interval's behavior from the description and explain why a resting
stretch is neither increasing nor decreasing.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.3, Rates of Change) puts a number on
today's ``gains'': the average rate of change over an interval --- computed
precisely, not just called growing or shrinking.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Items 1--2 are the 1.1 core ---
watch 2(b) again for students answering the solve with an output; if the 1.1
exit tickets flagged the direction confusion, this is the promised reteach
window. Item 2(c) is deliberately unlabeled vocabulary: students say
``the outputs go down'' in plain words today, and the lesson names it within
the half hour.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
Part 2's definition is the first inequality-quantified statement of the
course --- read $a < b \Rightarrow f(a) < f(b)$ aloud as a sentence
(``whenever one input beats another\ldots'') before writing it. In Part 3,
compute the gains in the margin next to each table \textit{live}; the
concavity call must visibly come from the arithmetic, not the picture. The
two bend sketches are pre-drawn --- students only label them. If time runs
short, Part 4 compresses to five minutes: the zero translation sentence is
the one non-negotiable; the practice box's zero item can move to homework
review tomorrow.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
All tiers read one graph, so regrouping mid-activity needs no new setup.
Tier R answers should be sentences with units (``at the start the trail is
100 meters up''). Tier A's concavity justification from the table gains is
the target skill --- listen for ``the gains shrink'' rather than ``it flattens.''
If a Tier E group nails the time-as-a-function-of-elevation flip, have them
present it in the closing minutes; it re-airs 1.1's many-to-one idea and
previews inverses in 1.6.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Scan item 1's sentence first: rate-of-change
language earns the check, shape language gets a margin note. Item 2 is a
straight vocabulary read --- a miss there means the word never landed and is
worth a warm-up slot tomorrow. Item 3's zero sentence should name the event
(``the pond is empty after 6 days''), not just restate ``the output is 0.''
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 5 is the AP-style multiple-choice item; distractor C (gains growing)
is the popular wrong answer and worth a two-minute autopsy tomorrow ---
it separates ``increasing'' from ``increasing faster.'' Problem 4 pairs with
the cocoa example from class; students who answered the cold-call in class
have already done it aloud. The extension's resting hiker introduces
``neither'' as a legitimate answer --- flag a good response to read aloud
before starting 1.3, since constant stretches return with average rate of
change.
\end{teachernote}
