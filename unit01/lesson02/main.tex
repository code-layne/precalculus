    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will describe how a function's output
    changes as its input increases; name the intervals where a function is
    increasing or decreasing; decide concavity from whether the rate of change
    is growing or shrinking; treat those two as \textit{independent} questions
    with four possible pairs of answers; and identify a zero and interpret it
    in context. \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topic 1.1 --- Skills 2.B, 3.A)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{2.B} --- Construct equivalent graphical, numerical, analytical, or
verbal representations of a function. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Input and output values vary in tandem according to the function
        rule. (1.1.A.1--2)
  \item A table shows a handful of input--output pairs; a graph shows every
        one at once, which is what makes trends visible. (1.1.B.1)
  \item A verbal description of two covarying quantities already determines
        the shape of the graph. (1.1.B.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function using
mathematical language and notation. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Increasing: whenever $a < b$, $f(a) < f(b)$. Decreasing: whenever
        $a < b$, $f(a) > f(b)$. Intervals are named with \textit{inputs}.
        (1.1.A.3--4)
  \item Concave up: the rate of change is increasing. Concave down: the rate
        of change is decreasing. (1.1.B.3--4)
  \item Direction and concavity are independent --- all four combinations
        occur.
  \item A zero is an input whose output is 0; the graph meets the horizontal
        axis there. (1.1.B.5)
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.24\linewidth} X @{}}
Vary in tandem  & Two quantities change together: each change in the input drives a change in the output, linked by the function rule. \\
Increasing      & On an interval, larger inputs give larger outputs: whenever $a < b$, $f(a) < f(b)$. The graph rises left to right. \\
Decreasing      & On an interval, larger inputs give smaller outputs: whenever $a < b$, $f(a) > f(b)$. The graph falls left to right. \\
Rate of change  & How much the output moves per step of input --- the ``gain'' (or loss) from one input to the next. Made precise in 1.3. \\
Concave up      & The rate of change is increasing on the interval: each step gains more, or loses less, than the step before. \\
Concave down    & The rate of change is decreasing on the interval: each step gains less, or loses more, than the step before. \\
Zero            & An input whose output equals 0; the graph meets the horizontal axis there. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: evaluating a function at a negative input (the
    parentheses habit from 1.1), reading a table in both directions ---
    find $p(2)$ versus solve $p(x) = 10$ --- and stating the domain and range
    of a function given as a list of points. Item 2(b) plants today's seed:
    students describe a falling column of outputs in plain English, with no
    vocabulary available yet.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{Two shows, same sales.} Project the two ticket-sales graphs from
    the guided notes. Both shows went on sale the same morning; six weeks
    later both have sold exactly 600 tickets.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Ask only this: if you could promote one of these shows for six
              more weeks, which one? Take a vote before any discussion.
        \item Then push on \textit{why}. Students will reach for ``it's still
              going up,'' ``it's flattening out,'' ``it's taking off'' ---
              catch those phrases and keep them on the board.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} both graphs are increasing and both end at
    600, so ``increasing'' cannot tell them apart. The thing students can see
    but cannot yet name is how the \textit{rate} of selling is changing. By
    the end of the hour it has a name: \textbf{concavity}.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: Two Quantities in Tandem} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item A hose fills a tank: as time $t$ increases, volume $V$ responds. $V$
        is a function of $t$ --- each moment has exactly one volume (1.0/1.1).
  \item Table: $t = 0, 2, 4, 6$ minutes gives $V = 0, 8, 16, 24$ gallons.
        Read it \textit{in tandem} --- every 2 minutes, 8 more gallons.
  \item Name the roles out loud: input quantity is time in minutes, output
        quantity is volume in gallons. Then the payoff line: a table shows a
        few pairs, a graph shows them all at once.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: Question 1 --- Which Way?} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Definition with teeth: increasing means whenever $a < b$,
        $f(a) < f(b)$. Read it aloud as a sentence before writing symbols.
        Decreasing flips the output inequality.
  \item Project the pre-drawn graph of $h$ (rise, fall, rise). Students name
        intervals with inequalities on the \textit{inputs}: increasing for
        $0 < x < 3$ and $6 < x < 8$, decreasing for $3 < x < 6$.
  \item Front-load two traps: intervals are named by $x$-values, never
        outputs; and decreasing $\ne$ negative --- cocoa falling from
        $90^\circ$ to $40^\circ$ never leaves positive territory.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: Question 2 --- How Is the Change Changing?} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Two tanks, both filling for 6 minutes, both ending at 24 gallons.
        Tank A: $0, 14, 20, 24$. Tank B: $0, 4, 10, 24$. Compute the gains
        \textit{live} in the margin --- 14, 6, 4 versus 4, 6, 14.
  \item Name them: A's rate of change is decreasing (concave down), B's is
        increasing (concave up). The call comes from the arithmetic, not the
        picture.
  \item Return to the hook: Show A was concave down, Show B concave up. That
        is the word they were missing an hour ago.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: The Four Corners} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Fill the four-row table together. Each row is a real situation, a
        pre-drawn curve, and the two answers: rumor (inc/up), warming soda
        (inc/down), cooling cocoa (dec/up), draining battery (dec/down).
  \item The point of the box, said explicitly: the two questions are
        \textit{independent}. Concave down does not mean falling.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 5: Zeros} (6 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item A zero is an input whose output is 0 --- so it is a \textit{solve}
        question from 1.1, not an evaluate question. Read the zeros of $h$ off
        the Part 2 graph ($x = 1$ and $x = 5$), then its domain and range.
  \item In context a zero is an event: the drone lands, the pond empties.
        Students translate $D(48) = 0$ into a sentence.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on the guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} naming intervals by outputs --- ``increasing
        from $-2$ to 3'' while pointing at $y$-values. Push: ``Increasing on
        which \textit{inputs}?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} decreasing confused with negative. Cold-call:
        ``Can a function be decreasing while every output is positive?''
        (Yes --- the cooling cocoa.)
  \item \textbf{Common error:} concavity welded to direction --- ``concave
        down means it's falling.'' Point at row 2 of the four corners: the
        warming soda is increasing \textit{and} concave down.
  \item \textbf{Justification check:} a concavity claim must cite the gains
        (``14, then 6, then 4''), not the shape (``it looks bendy'').
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Reservoir Year} in leveled
tiers. Every tier reads the same pre-drawn graph and its companion table ---
no tier draws anything.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Read $L(0)$ and $L(4)$ off the graph,
        circle increasing or decreasing on two named stretches, and translate
        the zero at $t = 9$ into a sentence about the town.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Name every interval with inequalities, fill
        in the monthly changes for the spring rise ($+4, +3, +2, +1$) and the
        fall refill ($+1, +2, +3$), and explain why two increasing stretches
        tell opposite stories.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Find the concavity of the summer drawdown
        from its changes ($-1$ down to $-5$), describe the one corner the year
        never visited (decreasing and concave up), and decide whether
        \textit{time} is a function of \textit{level} --- a 1.1 spiral.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item From a table of subscriber counts (gains 12, 9, 5), circle increasing
        or decreasing and concave up or down, then defend the concavity from
        the weekly changes.
  \item From a verbal description of cooling cocoa, circle both answers ---
        this is the decreasing-and-concave-up corner.
  \item Identify the zero of a pond-draining function and say what it means.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Item 1(b) is the hinge --- credit requires
rate-of-change language (``the gains are shrinking''), not shape talk. Item 2
is the diagnostic: a student who circles \textit{down} has welded concave down
to falling. Sort tickets by item 2 and reteach the four corners in the 1.3
warm-up window if more than a handful miss it.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems covering direction and concavity read straight
from a verbal description, a full graph read (intervals, zeros, domain and
range), a table-of-differences concavity call with justification, a concept
question (decreasing and concave up at once --- possible?), and one AP-style
multiple-choice item that identifies increasing-and-concave-down from a list
of successive changes.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} A hiker who rests mid-climb. Students decide
that a flat stretch is \textit{neither} increasing nor decreasing and name its
rate of change as 0 --- the constant case, which returns in 1.3.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.3, Rates of Change) puts a number on today's
``gains'': the average rate of change over an interval, computed from a
formula, a table, or a graph, with units.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Items 1 and 2(a) are the 1.1
core --- watch 2(a) again for students answering the solve with an output; if
the 1.1 exit tickets flagged that direction confusion, this is the promised
reteach window. Item 2(b) is deliberately vocabulary-free: students should
write something like ``they go down, but by less each time.'' Keep two or
three of those sentences to quote back during Part 3, when the class names
what they already described.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The hook is worth its five minutes --- take the vote before the discussion, or
the argument never happens. Part 2's definition is the first
inequality-quantified statement of the course; say ``whenever one input beats
another\ldots'' aloud before writing $a < b$. In Part 3 compute the gains in
the margin \textit{live} so the concavity call visibly comes from arithmetic,
and only then show that it matches the picture. Part 4 is the spine of the
lesson: fill all four rows, and finish by saying the independence claim out
loud. Part 5 reuses the Part 2 graph on purpose --- do not put up a new
figure. If time runs short, Part 5 compresses to four minutes and the practice
box's second item moves to homework review; do not cut Part 4.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
All tiers read one graph and one table, so groups can be re-formed mid-period
with no new setup. Tier R answers should be sentences with units (``in month 9
the water is level with the outlet valve''). Tier A's items 2 and 3 are the
target skill --- the two change rows are designed to be mirror images, so a
group that fills both correctly has the whole idea. Listen for ``the gains
shrink'' rather than ``it flattens.'' Tier E item 1 is the one students most
often get backwards: the drawdown is decreasing \textit{and} concave down,
because $-1, -2, -3, -4, -5$ is a falling rate of change. If a group nails the
time-as-a-function-of-level flip, have them present it in the closing minutes;
it re-airs 1.1's one-to-many idea and previews inverses in 1.6.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Scan item 2 first --- it is the fastest
read and the sharpest diagnostic, since ``decreasing and concave up'' is the
corner class time covers least. Then read item 1(b): rate-of-change language
earns the check, shape language earns a margin note. Item 3's sentence should
name the event (``the pond is empty after six days''), not restate ``the
output is 0.''
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 5 is the AP-style multiple choice; distractor A ($+2, +4, +6, +8$) is
the popular wrong answer and separates ``increasing'' from ``increasing
faster'' --- worth a two-minute autopsy tomorrow. Problem 1(b), the
parachutist, is the same corner as exit-ticket item 2, so a student who missed
one and got the other is worth a quick conversation. Problem 4 pairs with the
cocoa cold-call from class. The extension introduces ``neither'' as a
legitimate answer and names a rate of change of 0; flag a good response to
read aloud at the start of 1.3, since constant stretches come back
immediately.
\end{teachernote}
