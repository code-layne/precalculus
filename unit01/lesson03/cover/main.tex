\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item I can explain what the average rate of change over an interval
        promises: the one steady rate that would produce the same change in
        output over the same run.
  \item I can compute an average rate of change from a formula, from a table,
        and from a graph, and I always attach units.
  \item I can read the sign of a rate of change as a statement about how two
        quantities move together, and I know what a rate of change of zero
        does \textit{not} say.
  \item I can recover detail the average erased by computing it over smaller
        intervals.
  \item I can use a list of average rates of change to identify a linear or
        quadratic function and to name concavity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small
Last lesson you said a function's changes were ``growing'' or ``shrinking'' by
comparing them. Today you put a number on them. That number is a
\textit{stand-in}: the one steady rate that would have done the same job over
the same stretch. It is powerful precisely because it ignores everything
between the endpoints --- and when you need that detail back, you compute it
again over smaller pieces.
\end{remindbox}
