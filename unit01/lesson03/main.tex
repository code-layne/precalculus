\medskip
\textbf{MP 3.C} --- Support conclusions with a logical rationale or appropriate data. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item For a linear function, the AROC is constant over \textit{any} interval. (1.3.A.1)
  \item For a quadratic function, successive equal-length AROCs form a linear pattern. (1.3.A.2)
  \item The AROC over $[a,b]$ equals the slope of the secant line through $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$. (1.3.A.3)
  \item Linear functions: the AROCs change at a rate of zero (second differences $= 0$). (1.3.B.1)
  \item Quadratic functions: the AROCs change at a constant nonzero rate (second differences constant). (1.3.B.2)
  \item Increasing AROCs $\Rightarrow$ concave up; decreasing AROCs $\Rightarrow$ concave down. (1.3.B.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{sky}
    We computed one AROC last lesson.
    \textbf{What if we compute many in a row for $f(x) = x^2$?}

    \smallskip
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
    \hline
    $x$    & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
    \hline
    $f(x)$ & 0 & 1 & 4 & 9 & 16 \\
    \hline
    \end{tabular}

    \smallskip
    Compute the AROC on $[0,1]$, $[1,2]$, $[2,3]$, $[3,4]$.
    \textit{Discussion:} ``What pattern do you notice in those four numbers?
    What do you think the AROC on $[4,5]$ will be?''

    \medskip
    Use student responses to motivate the ideas of \emph{first differences} and \emph{second differences}.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{sky}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: AROC of Linear Functions} (10 min)

Use $f(x) = 2x + 1$. Build a table together on the board:

\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
Interval & $\Delta f$ & AROC \\
\hline
$[0,1]$ & $3-1=2$ & 2 \\
$[1,2]$ & $5-3=2$ & 2 \\
$[2,3]$ & $7-5=2$ & 2 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\smallskip

Ask: \textbf{``Why is the AROC always the same for a linear function?''}
(The slope of the secant line between any two points on a line is the slope of the line.)

Second differences = $2 - 2 = 0$: linear $\Rightarrow$ zero second differences.

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 2: AROC of Quadratic Functions} (12 min)

Use $f(x) = x^2$. First differences (AROCs over unit intervals):
$1, 3, 5, 7, \ldots$ --- ask: \textbf{``What kind of pattern is this?''}

Second differences: $3-1=2$, $5-3=2$, $7-5=2$ --- constant.

\textit{Key takeaway:} For a quadratic function the first differences form a linear (arithmetic) sequence; the second differences are constant and nonzero.

\textbf{Part 3: Secant Lines \& Concavity} (8 min)

Secant line through $(1,1)$ and $(3,9)$: slope $= \frac{9-1}{3-1} = 4$.
This is exactly the AROC on $[1,3]$.

\textbf{Concavity connection:} Since the first differences of $f(x)=x^2$ are increasing ($1 < 3 < 5 < 7$), the graph is \emph{concave up}. If first differences were decreasing, the graph would be concave down.

\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on guided notes and the activity, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} Students compute $f(b) - f(a)$ but forget to divide by $b - a$.
        Cold-call: ``The change in output from $x=2$ to $x=4$ is 12. What is the AROC?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} Students think a quadratic AROC is constant (confusing with linear).
        Ask: ``Are your first differences for $x^2$ the same each time? What about the \emph{differences} of those differences?''
  \item \textbf{Notation:} Watch for students writing ``AROC = 3, 5, 7'' without labeling intervals.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call:} ``How do you know from a table alone whether a function is linear or quadratic?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: Falling Ball} in leveled tiers.
All tiers use height data from $h(t) = 144 - 16t^2$ at half-second intervals.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Compute the AROC of height over each 0.5-second interval.
        Identify whether AROCs are increasing or decreasing. Interpret what this says about the ball's speed.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Compute first differences and second differences. Show second differences are constant.
        Identify the function type from this pattern.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Use the constant second difference to predict the AROC on $[3.0, 3.5]$
        without computing from the formula. Verify. Connect constant second differences to constant acceleration.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A table of values for $h(x)$: compute consecutive AROCs, check second differences, identify function type.
  \item For a linear function with AROC $= 4$ on $[2,5]$: state the AROC on $[5,10]$ and justify.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Look for students explaining \textit{why} the AROC is constant for linear functions
(not just observing that it is). Quadratic identification should cite constant second differences, not just guess.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems --- constant AROC verification for a linear function, first and second differences
for a quadratic, table-based function-type identification, concavity reasoning from AROC patterns, and one
AP-style MC using a table of values.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} Students explore $f(x) = x^3$ and compute first, second, and third differences,
predicting what function type governs the second differences.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson, we study polynomial functions more broadly and connect a polynomial's degree
to the number of differences needed to reach a constant.
\end{skillbox}
