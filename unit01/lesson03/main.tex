    interpret its sign and units in context; and use the pattern of average
    rates of change to tell linear functions from quadratic ones and to name
    concavity. \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topics 1.2 \& 1.3 --- Skills 2.A, 3.A, 3.B)}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{2.A} --- Identify information from graphical, numerical, analytical,
or verbal representations to answer a question. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item AROC over $[a,b]$ is $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$: the one constant rate
        that yields the same net change the function produced. (1.2.A.1)
  \item Geometrically, AROC is the slope of the secant line through
        $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. (1.3.A.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A positive rate of change means the two quantities move in the same
        direction; a negative one means opposite directions. (1.2.B.2--3)
  \item Rates carry units: output units per input unit. (1.2.B.1)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.B} --- Apply numerical results in context. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Linear: AROC is constant over every interval. Quadratic: AROCs over
        consecutive equal-length intervals change at a constant rate.
        (1.3.A.1--2, 1.3.B.1--2)
  \item AROCs increasing $\Rightarrow$ concave up; AROCs decreasing
        $\Rightarrow$ concave down --- the precise version of 1.2's bending
        language. (1.3.B.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Rate of change   & How fast the output changes with respect to the input, measured in output units per input unit. \\
Average rate of change (AROC) & Over $[a,b]$: $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ --- the constant rate that would produce the same net change in output over the interval. \\
Secant line      & The line through two points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ on a graph; its slope is the AROC over $[a,b]$. \\
Positive / negative rate & Positive: the quantities rise or fall together. Negative: as one increases, the other decreases. \\
First differences & The AROCs over consecutive equal-length input intervals --- the "steps" in a table with equally spaced inputs. \\
Concave up / down (from 1.2) & AROCs increasing $\Rightarrow$ concave up; AROCs decreasing $\Rightarrow$ concave down. \\
\end{tabularx}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{The road-trip average.} A family drives 120 miles in 3 hours and
    the GPS reports: \textit{average speed 40 mph}.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Ask: were they going exactly 40 the whole time? (Traffic, gas
              stop, open highway --- of course not.)
        \item Push for what ``average'' \textit{promises}: one steady speed of
              40 mph would have covered the \textit{same 120 miles in the same
              3 hours}.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} that promise is today's definition. The
    average rate of change is the one constant rate that gets the same net
    change done --- and unlike the speedometer, we can compute it for
    \textit{any} function from just two points.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: AROC = Slope, Upgraded} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Recall the slope formula on the warm-up's two points --- then rename:
        for a function, $\text{AROC} = \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$, change in
        output over change in input.
  \item Example: $f(x) = x^2$ on $[1, 4]$ gives AROC $= 5$. The curve is not
        a line --- 5 is the slope of the \textbf{secant line} through
        $(1,1)$ and $(4,16)$.
  \item Anchor the meaning: 5 is the constant rate that produces the same net
        change ($+15$) over the same run ($3$). Connect to the hook's GPS.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: AROC from a Table and a Graph} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Table of temperatures: compute AROC over $[0,2]$, $[2,4]$, $[4,6]$;
        ask which stretch warmed fastest and how they know.
  \item Project the pre-drawn hill graph from the notes. Read coordinates,
        compute AROC on $[0,4]$ ($=1$) and on $[4,8]$ ($=-1$); the dashed
        secant makes ``slope between two points'' visible.
  \item Watch the subtraction order: $f(b)-f(a)$ upstairs, $b-a$ downstairs,
        \textit{same order both floors}.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: What the Sign Says} (8 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Positive AROC: the quantities move together (climbing temperature).
        Negative: opposite directions (cooling after sunset). Zero: no net
        change --- flag that it does \textit{not} mean nothing happened.
  \item Units discipline: every AROC is output units per input unit ---
        $^\circ$F per hour, feet per second. Require the units in every
        spoken answer from here on.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: The Pattern of the AROCs --- Linear vs.\ Quadratic} (14 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item $f(x) = 2x+1$: AROCs over $[0,1], [1,2], [2,3], [3,4]$ are
        $2, 2, 2, 2$. Linear means the AROC never changes --- that is what
        ``slope'' being one number means.
  \item $g(x) = x^2$: AROCs are $1, 3, 5, 7$ --- not constant, but climbing
        by exactly $2$ each step. Quadratic means the AROCs themselves change
        at a constant rate.
  \item Close the loop with 1.2: AROCs increasing $\Rightarrow$ concave up
        (the parabola bends upward); AROCs decreasing $\Rightarrow$ concave
        down. Yesterday's ``bending'' now has a computation behind it.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} subtraction order flipped in one floor of the
        fraction --- $f(a)-f(b)$ over $b-a$. Cold-call: ``Which output goes
        first? Then which input goes first?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} reporting the net change ($f(b)-f(a)$ alone)
        as the rate. Ask: ``Per what? Where did the divide go?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} calling a negative AROC ``no change'' or
        ``slower.'' Push: ``Negative says \textit{direction}, not speed ---
        which quantity is falling?''
  \item \textbf{Units:} answers without units are incomplete. ``Three
        \textit{what} per \textit{what}?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Water Balloon Launch} in
leveled tiers. Every tier reads the same table and pre-drawn parabola --- no
tier sketches anything.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Scaffolded AROC computations over
        $[0,1]$ and $[1,2]$ with labeled blanks, then ``which second did it
        climb faster?'' and a rising/falling sign read.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Fill the full AROC table ($48, 16, -16,
        -48$), interpret the $-16$ in a sentence with units, spot the
        constant $-32$ step, and explain why AROC $=0$ on $[0,4]$ is
        technically right but misleading.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Shrink the interval at $t=1$ ---
        AROCs over $[1,2]$, $[1,1.5]$, $[1,1.1]$ from supplied values close
        in on the balloon's speed \textit{at} the instant $t=1$
        (CED 1.2.A.2, exposure only), plus ``when is the rate zero?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the AROC of $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ over $[1, 3]$ (worked space
        provided).
  \item From a temperature table, compute the AROC over $[0, 4]$ and write
        one sentence interpreting its sign and units.
  \item Two functions' AROC lists over equal steps ($5,5,5,5$ vs.\
        $2,5,8,11$): identify the linear one, and the concavity of the other.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Item 2 is the interpretation check --- a correct
$-3$ with no units or no direction sentence means Part 3 needs a warm-up
revisit tomorrow. Item 3 requires no arithmetic at all; a miss there is a
concept miss on the lesson's big idea, not a computation slip.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems covering AROC from a formula (two intervals),
AROC from a population table with a fastest-decade comparison, a
sign-and-units interpretation sentence, an AROC-pattern table that classifies
a function and its concavity, and one AP-style multiple-choice AROC item.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} For $f(x) = x^2$, AROCs over $[3, 3.1]$ and
$[3, 3.01]$ (values supplied) creep toward 6 --- the rate of change
\textit{at} $x = 3$. A first, informal look at the idea calculus makes exact.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.4, Transformations of Functions) leaves
rates for shapes: what happens to a graph when we shift it, flip it, or
stretch it --- and how the formula records each move.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1 is the load-bearing one:
its slope computation returns in Part 1 renamed as AROC, so a student who
fumbles the subtraction order here will fumble it all lesson --- note names.
Item 2 keeps the parentheses habit alive; item 3's table read (falling
outputs, zero at $x=6$) is the 1.2 language today's sign discussion builds on.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
Part 1: write the slope formula and the AROC formula side by side and let
students see they are the same fraction wearing function notation --- ``slope,
upgraded'' is the phrase to repeat. The secant line on the hill graph is
pre-drawn; students only read coordinates off it. Part 4 is the absorbed
Topic 1.3 material at exposure depth: the goal is the \textit{pattern}
($2,2,2,2$ vs.\ $1,3,5,7$) and the concavity link, not second-difference
drill. If time runs short, compress Part 3 into Part 2's table discussion ---
do not cut Part 4, it is the exit ticket's item 3.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
All three tiers read the same table and parabola, so groups can be regrouped
mid-activity without new setup. Tier R must say answers with units aloud
(``48 feet per second''). Tier A's AROC $= 0$ on $[0,4]$ question is the
hinge --- the balloon went up 64 feet and came back, and the average erases
the whole story; that insight is worth a whole-class share in the closing
minutes. Tier E's shrinking-interval table previews instantaneous rate:
keep it informal, ``the AROCs are closing in on 32,'' and resist the word
``limit'' unless a student produces it.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Scan item 2 first: the diagnostic miss is
a bare ``$-3$'' with no per-hour and no falling-temperature sentence ---
sort those for a reteach. On item 3, accept ``concave up'' or ``bends
upward'' for function B; the point is that they classified from the AROC
list alone, without a graph.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 5 is the AP-style item; distractor B is the net change with the
divide dropped and distractor C uses the whole table's endpoints instead of
the named interval --- both are worth two minutes of autopsy tomorrow. The
extension is the only instantaneous-rate item outside Tier E; if the class
never reached the Tier E discussion, assign it explicitly and open 1.4's
warm-up window with it.
\end{teachernote}
