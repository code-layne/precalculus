    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will read the average rate of change
    over an interval as the one \textit{steady} rate that would have done the
    same job; compute it from a formula, a table, or a graph, with units;
    interpret its sign; recognize that it erases everything between the
    endpoints and recover that detail by computing it over smaller intervals;
    and use the resulting list of rates to identify linear and quadratic
    behavior and to name concavity.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topics 1.2 \& 1.3 --- Skills 2.A, 3.A, 3.B, 3.C)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{2.A} --- Identify information from graphical, numerical, analytical,
or verbal representations to answer a question. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item The average rate of change over an interval is the \textit{constant}
        rate that yields the same change in output the function actually
        produced there. (1.2.A.1)
  \item In symbols, $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$: change in output over change in
        input, read off a formula, a table, or a graph.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function using
mathematical language and notation. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A rate of change quantifies how two quantities vary together.
        (1.2.B.1)
  \item Positive: the quantities move the same way. Negative: as one
        increases, the other decreases. (1.2.B.2--3)
  \item Geometrically the AROC is the slope of the \textbf{secant line}
        through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ --- the stand-in's own graph.
        (1.3.A.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.B} --- Apply numerical results in a given applied context. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Every rate carries units: output units \textit{per} input unit.
  \item Averaging over a whole interval discards what happened inside it;
        averaging over small intervals near a point recovers the rate
        \textit{at} that point. (1.2.A.2--3, exposure only)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{3.C} --- Support a conclusion with a logical rationale or
appropriate data. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Linear: the AROC is the same over every interval, so the AROCs change
        at a rate of zero. (1.3.A.1, 1.3.B.1)
  \item Quadratic: the AROCs over consecutive equal-length intervals march by
        a constant step --- they are themselves linear. (1.3.A.2, 1.3.B.2)
  \item AROCs increasing $\Rightarrow$ concave up; AROCs decreasing
        $\Rightarrow$ concave down --- the numerical version of 1.2's bending
        language. (1.3.B.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Average rate of change & Over $[a,b]$: \TallMath{\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}} --- the one constant rate that would carry the function from $(a,f(a))$ to $(b,f(b))$ over the same run. \\
Secant line      & The straight line through two points of a graph. Its slope \textit{is} the average rate of change over that interval. \\
Positive rate of change & As the input increases the output increases too --- the two quantities move the same way. \\
Negative rate of change & As the input increases the output decreases --- the two quantities move opposite ways. \\
Constant rate of change & The same average rate over every interval. This is exactly what makes a function linear; that number is its slope. \\
Rate of change at a point & What the average rates over shorter and shorter intervals around the point close in on. Exposure only in this course. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: slope from two points --- the exact arithmetic today's
    definition runs on, renamed; evaluating a function at two inputs with the
    parentheses habit from 1.1, using the same $f(x) = x^2 + 3$ and the same
    inputs 1 and 4 that open Part 2; and a 1.2 table read for direction and
    for whether the changes are growing or shrinking. Nothing on the warm-up
    is new --- every item is a piece of a Part 1 or Part 2 computation, handed
    back one step at a time.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{Sixty steps, thirty seconds.} Project the two stair-climb tables
    from the guided notes. Ravi and Mira both start at the bottom of the same
    60-step stadium stairway and both reach the top exactly 30 seconds later.
    Ravi sprints, fades, and trudges (40, then 15, then 5 steps per 10-second
    split); Mira climbs steadily (20, 20, 20).
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Take a vote first: who climbed \textit{faster}? Do not let anyone
              justify it yet.
        \item Then compute the average for each --- both come out to exactly
              \textbf{2 steps per second}. The number the class was reaching
              for calls them identical.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} the average is not wrong and it is not lying.
    It answers a narrower question than the class asked: \textit{what one
    steady rate would have gotten the same person from the bottom to the top
    in the same 30 seconds?} It is a \textbf{stand-in}, and a stand-in has no
    opinion about the middle. Today: how to compute the stand-in, and what to
    do when you want the middle back.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: The Stand-In} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Say the definition in words before symbols: the average rate of change
        over $[a,b]$ is the one \textit{constant} rate that produces the same
        change in output over the same change in input.
  \item Then the formula, built from the climb: $60$ steps over $30$ seconds,
        so $\text{AROC} = \frac{60-0}{30-0} = 2$ steps per second --- for both
        climbers. Write $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ underneath and match the pieces.
  \item Name what it costs: the stand-in is pinned only at the two endpoints.
        Everything between them is deliberately thrown away.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: Computing It Three Ways} (14 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{From a formula:} $f(x) = x^2 + 3$ on $[1,4]$. The warm-up
        already produced $f(1)=4$ and $f(4)=19$; the AROC is $15/3 = 5$.
  \item \textbf{From a table:} the morning-temperature table. AROCs over
        $[0,2]$, $[2,4]$, $[6,8]$ come out $3$, $5$, $-1$ $^\circ$F per hour.
        Ask which stretch warmed fastest and how they know.
  \item \textbf{From a graph:} the pre-drawn graph of $g$ with its two dashed
        secant lines. Read the endpoints, compute $+1$ and $-1$, then say the
        punch line: the AROC \textit{is} the slope of that dashed line.
  \item Subtraction order, out loud: outputs on top in the same order as the
        inputs on the bottom.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: What the Sign Says} (7 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Positive: the two quantities move together. Negative: they move
        opposite ways. Insist on units in every spoken answer from here on ---
        ``three \textit{what} per \textit{what}?''
  \item Zero is the interesting one: a hiker who ends at the elevation she
        started at has AROC $0$ for the afternoon. The stand-in stands still;
        the hiker did not. This is Part 1's cost made visible.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: Chop It Finer} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Return to Ravi and Mira and compute the AROC of each 10-second split:
        Ravi $4$, $1.5$, $0.5$; Mira $2$, $2$, $2$. The vote is settled ---
        with smaller intervals the story comes back.
  \item Ravi's rates are \textit{shrinking}; in 1.2 language his climb is
        concave down. Mira's never change at all. Same tool, finer grain.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 5: The Pattern Names the Function} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item $f(x) = 2x+1$ over the four unit intervals from $0$ to $4$: AROCs
        $2,2,2,2$. Constant AROC \textit{is} what linear means, and that
        number is the slope.
  \item $g(x) = x^2$ over the same intervals: $1, 3, 5, 7$ --- not constant,
        but climbing by exactly $2$ every time. A quadratic's rates are
        themselves linear.
  \item Close the loop with 1.2: rates increasing $\Rightarrow$ concave up;
        rates decreasing $\Rightarrow$ concave down. Yesterday's ``the gains
        are shrinking'' now has a computation under it.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on the guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} subtraction order flipped on one floor of the
        fraction --- $f(a)-f(b)$ over $b-a$. Cold-call: ``Which output did you
        write first? Then which input has to go first?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} reporting the net change as the rate. Ask:
        ``Per what? Where did the dividing go?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} reading a negative AROC as ``slower'' or as
        ``no change.'' Push: ``Negative reports \textit{direction}. Which
        quantity is falling?''
  \item \textbf{Spine check:} when a student gets AROC $=0$ on the hiker and
        writes ``nothing happened,'' stop the group. That sentence is the
        misconception the whole lesson is built to break.
  \item \textbf{Units:} an answer without units is unfinished, every time.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The Rocket Club Launch} in
leveled tiers. Every tier reads the same table and the same pre-drawn
parabola --- no tier draws anything.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} Read two heights off the table, compute
        the AROC over $[0,1]$ and over $[4,5]$ into labeled blanks
        ($80$ and $-48$ ft per second), and say which second of the climb was
        faster.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Apply):} Fill the whole one-second AROC row
        ($80, 48, 16, -16, -48, -80$), interpret the $-16$ in a sentence with
        units, notice the constant $-32$ step from one rate to the next and
        name the function type, then confront AROC $=0$ over $[0,6]$ --- true,
        and it erases a 144-foot flight.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extend):} Given AROCs over $[1,2]$, $[1,1.5]$,
        $[1,1.1]$, $[1,1.01]$ ($48, 56, 62.4, 63.84$), say what number they
        are closing in on and what it measures (CED 1.2.A.2, exposure only);
        locate the instant the rate is zero; and decide whether \textit{time}
        is a function of \textit{height} --- a 1.1 spiral.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the AROC of $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ over $[1,3]$, with work space.
  \item From a hiker's elevation table that ends where it started: report the
        AROC over the whole afternoon ($0$ ft per hour), then say in one
        sentence what that $0$ hides --- and back it up with the AROC over the
        first hour.
  \item From two lists of AROCs over equal steps ($6,6,6,6$ versus
        $1,4,7,10$): name the linear one and the concavity of the other.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} item 2 is the lesson. A student who writes
``nothing happened'' or leaves the justification blank has the arithmetic and
not the idea, and should be first in the 1.4 warm-up window. Item 3 needs no
arithmetic at all --- a miss there is conceptual, not a slip.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems --- AROC from a formula over two intervals with
opposite signs, AROC from a population table with a fastest-decade comparison
and units, a graph read with two pre-drawn secant lines plus a sign
interpretation, an AROC-pattern table that classifies two functions and names
a concavity, and one AP-style multiple-choice AROC item.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} For $f(x) = x^2$, the AROCs over $[3, 3.5]$,
$[3, 3.1]$, and $[3, 3.01]$ (values supplied) run $6.5$, $6.1$, $6.01$.
Students name what they are closing in on and what it would measure --- the
first informal look at the quantity calculus makes exact.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (1.4, Transformations of Functions) leaves rates
for shapes: what shifting, flipping, and stretching a graph does, and how the
formula records each move.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1 is load-bearing: its slope
computation returns twenty minutes later renamed as the average rate of
change, so a student who fumbles the subtraction order here will fumble it all
period --- note names as you circulate. Item 2 hands back $f(1)$ and $f(4)$
for $f(x)=x^2+3$; Part 2 picks those two values up untouched, so students see
the AROC assembled from work they already did. Item 3 is the 1.2 read
(decreasing, changes shrinking in size, concave up) that Part 4 converts from
words into numbers.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
Take the hook vote before any computing --- if the class does not commit to an
answer first, the average coming out equal for both climbers lands as an
arithmetic fact instead of a provocation. Say ``stand-in'' every time you say
``average rate of change'' for the first twenty minutes; the phrase is doing
the conceptual work all period, and it is what makes Part 3's zero and the
activity's hinge feel inevitable rather than paradoxical. In Part 2 do the
three representations in the printed order and stop at the graph long enough
to point at the dashed line and say it \textit{is} the answer. Part 5 is the
absorbed Topic 1.3 material at exposure depth: the target is the pattern
($2,2,2,2$ against $1,3,5,7$) and the concavity link, not second-difference
drill. If time runs short, fold Part 3 into the Part 2 table discussion --- do
not cut Part 4, which is what makes Part 5 make sense.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
All three tiers read the same table and parabola, so groups can be re-formed
mid-period with no new setup. Require Tier R to say answers aloud with units
(``eighty feet per second''); the negative in item 3 is the first signed rate
most of them will have interpreted. Tier A item 4 is the hinge of the whole
lesson --- the rocket climbed 144 feet and came back, and the average over the
full flight reports zero. Give it a whole-class share in the closing minutes
even if no other tier work gets aired. Tier E's shrinking-interval table
previews the rate at a point: keep it informal (``these are closing in on
64''), and do not introduce the word \textit{limit} unless a student says it
first.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Read item 2 first --- it is the fastest
scan and the sharpest diagnostic. A correct $0$ with an empty or hand-waving
sentence is the miss that matters; the sentence has to name what the average
threw away and cite the $+500$ ft per hour first-hour rate as evidence. Item 1
is arithmetic and should be near-universal; if it is not, the subtraction
order is the culprit, not the concept. On item 3 accept ``bends upward'' for
``concave up'' --- the point is that they classified from a list of rates with
no graph in front of them.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 1 is deliberately two intervals of opposite sign on the same function,
so a student cannot pattern-match one answer onto the other. Problem 5 is the
AP-style item: distractor A is the net change with the dividing dropped,
distractor C uses the table's outer endpoints instead of the named interval,
and distractor D is the subtraction order reversed --- all three are worth two
minutes of autopsy tomorrow, since each names a specific error from Active
Monitoring. The extension is the only rate-at-a-point item outside Tier E; if
the class never reached that discussion, assign it explicitly and open the 1.4
warm-up window with a student's answer.
\end{teachernote}
