\begin{objectivebox}
By the end of this lesson, I will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \ansline{Determine the AROC of a linear function and explain why it is constant.}
  \item \ansline{Compute first and second differences for a quadratic function and describe the pattern.}
  \item \ansline{Use the AROC pattern to determine whether a graph is concave up or concave down.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\begin{vocabbox}
\termblanklong{secant line}
\ansline{The line through two points on a graph, $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$; its slope equals the AROC on $[a,b]$.}

\termblanklong{first differences}
\ansline{The successive AROCs computed over consecutive equal-length intervals.}

\termblanklong{second differences}
\ansline{The differences of consecutive first differences; constant for quadratic functions.}
\end{vocabbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\begin{hookbox}
We computed one AROC last lesson. What if we compute many AROCs in a row for $f(x) = x^2$?

\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$    & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
\hline
$f(x)$ & 0 & 1 & 4 & 9 & 16 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
What pattern do you notice in the successive AROCs?

\ansline{The AROCs are 1, 3, 5, 7 --- they increase by 2 each time (an arithmetic pattern).}
\ansline{The second differences are all 2, which is constant.}
\end{hookbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\begin{notesbox}{AROC of Linear Functions}
Use $f(x) = 2x + 1$. Complete the table.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Interval} & $f(\text{left})$ & $f(\text{right})$ & \textbf{AROC} \\
\hline
$[0, 1]$ & $f(0) = 1$ & $f(1) = 3$ & \ans{2} \\
$[1, 2]$ & $f(1) = 3$ & $f(2) = 5$ & \ans{2} \\
$[2, 3]$ & $f(2) = 5$ & $f(3) = 7$ & \ans{2} \\
$[3, 4]$ & $f(3) = 7$ & $f(4) = 9$ & \ans{2} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1}

\smallskip
\textbf{Conclusion:} For a linear function, the AROC is \ans{constant (always equal to the slope)}.

This is because the slope of any secant line on a linear graph equals \ans{the slope of the line itself}.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\begin{notesbox}{AROC of Quadratic Functions}
Use $f(x) = x^2$. Complete the table and the second-differences column.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Interval} & \textbf{AROC (first difference)} & \textbf{Change in AROC (second difference)} \\
\hline
$[0, 1]$ & \ans{1} & \\
$[1, 2]$ & \ans{3} & \ans{2} \\
$[2, 3]$ & \ans{5} & \ans{2} \\
$[3, 4]$ & \ans{7} & \ans{2} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1}

\smallskip
\textbf{Conclusion:} For a quadratic function, the second differences are \ans{constant (nonzero)}.

The first differences form a \ans{linear (arithmetic)} pattern.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\begin{notesbox}{Secant Lines \& Concavity}
\textbf{Secant line:} The line connecting two points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ on a graph.

Its slope $= \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} =$ \ans{the AROC on $[a,b]$}

\medskip
\textbf{Concavity from AROC pattern:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item If the AROCs are \textit{increasing} as $x$ increases, the graph is \ans{concave up}.
  \item If the AROCs are \textit{decreasing} as $x$ increases, the graph is \ans{concave down}.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textit{Example:} For $f(x) = x^2$, the AROCs over unit intervals are $1, 3, 5, 7, \ldots$

These are \ans{increasing}, so the graph is \ans{concave up}.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\begin{practicebox}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=8pt]

\item For $g(x) = -x^2 + 4x$, compute the AROC on $[0,1]$, $[1,2]$, $[2,3]$, $[3,4]$.
Then compute the second differences. Is the graph concave up or concave down?

\medskip
AROCs: $g(0)=0, g(1)=3, g(2)=4, g(3)=3, g(4)=0$.

AROC on $[0,1]$: \ans{3}; $[1,2]$: \ans{1}; $[2,3]$: \ans{$-1$}; $[3,4]$: \ans{$-3$}.

Second differences: \ans{$-2$} (constant). Graph is \ans{concave down}.

\item A function $p(x)$ has AROC values $12, 9, 6, 3$ on consecutive equal-length intervals.
Are the second differences positive or negative? What does this tell you about the concavity?

\medskip
Second differences: \ans{$-3$} (negative). The AROCs are decreasing, so the graph is \ans{concave down}.

\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}
