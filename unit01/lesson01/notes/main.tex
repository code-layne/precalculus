\begin{notesbox}{1. Function Notation --- Naming the Machine}
A function has three parts, and the notation $f(x)$ names all three at once:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item $f$ is the \blank{3cm} of the function (the machine itself),
  \item $x$ is the \blank{3cm} (what goes in the slot),
  \item $f(x)$ is the \blank{3cm} (what comes out).
\end{itemize}

$f(x)$ is read ``$f$ \blank{1.4cm} $x$.'' It is \textbf{not} $f$ times $x$ ---
those parentheses hold the input, they do not multiply.

\medskip
\textbf{In context.} A rideshare app charges a flat fee plus a rate per mile:
$F(m) = 2.50 + 1.75m$.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item The machine's name is \blank{1.4cm}; the input $m$ stands for
        \blank{3.4cm}; the output $F(m)$ stands for \blank{3.6cm}.
  \item So $F(4)$ asks: what does a \blank{1.2cm}-mile ride cost?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{One statement, three facts.} The single statement $f(3) = 7$ says:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item the input is \blank{1.4cm}, \quad the output is \blank{1.4cm},
  \item and the point ( \blank{1.2cm} , \blank{1.2cm} ) is on the graph of $f$.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Evaluating from a Formula}
To \textbf{evaluate} a function, drop the input into the slot ---
\textit{always inside parentheses} --- and simplify. The slot does not care what
you put in it:
\[ f(x) = 5x - 8 \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad f(\square) = 5(\square) - 8 \]

\textbf{Example 1.} Find $f(3)$.

\begin{work}
  f(3) &= 5(3) - 8 \\
       &= 7
\end{work}

\textbf{Example 2.} Find $f(-2)$.

\begin{work}
  f(-2) &= 5(-2) - 8 \\
        &= -18
\end{work}

\textbf{Example 3.} Let $g(x) = x^2 + 3x$. Find $g(-4)$.

\begin{work}
  g(-4) &= (-4)^2 + 3(-4) \\
        &= 16 - 12 \\
        &= 4
\end{work}

\textbf{Watch the trap:} $(-4)^2 = $ \blank{1.4cm}, but $-4^2 = $
\blank{1.4cm}. Writing the parentheses \textit{first}, empty, is what saves you.

\medskip
Evaluating never changes the rule --- it just runs the machine \blank{1.6cm}.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. Reading Both Directions off a Graph}
Nothing new happens here --- the two directions are the same. The only change is
that the input--output pairs come off a picture instead of a row.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.6cm, y=0.5cm]
  \draw[linegray, very thin, step=1] (0,0) grid (8,7);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (8.6,0) node[below right] {\small $x$};
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (0,7.6) node[above] {\small $f(x)$};
  \foreach \x in {1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8} \node[below] at (\x,0) {\small \x};
  \foreach \y in {1,3,5,7} \node[left] at (0,\y) {\small \y};
  \draw[very thick, plum] (0,5) -- (2,1);
  \draw[very thick, plum] (2,1) -- (5,7);
  \draw[very thick, plum] (5,7) -- (8,4);
  \fill[plum] (0,5) circle (2.5pt);
  \fill[plum] (8,4) circle (2.5pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item $f(2) = $ \blank{1.4cm} \quad (start at $x = 2$, walk \textit{up} to the
        graph, read across)
  \item $f(6) = $ \blank{1.4cm}
  \item Solve $f(x) = 3$: \quad $x = $ \blank{1.2cm} and $x = $ \blank{1.2cm}
        \quad (start at 3 on the \textit{output} axis, walk across)
  \item Solve $f(x) = 5$: \quad $x = $ \blank{1cm}, \blank{1cm}, and
        \blank{1cm} \quad --- three inputs share one output, and $f$ is
        \textit{still} a function. (Many-to-one, from Lesson 1.0.)
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{5. Domain and Range}
The \textbf{domain} is the set of all \blank{1.8cm} a function uses; the
\textbf{range} is the set of all \blank{1.8cm} it produces. A repeated output
is listed \blank{1.4cm}.

\medskip
\textbf{From a list of points.} Let $f = \{(-2, 6),\ (0, 4),\ (3, 6),\ (5, 1)\}$.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item Domain: \blank{4cm} \quad Range: \blank{4cm}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{From a graph.} Look back at the graph in Part 4: it runs from $x = 0$ to
$x = 8$ left to right, and its heights run from $1$ up to $7$.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item Domain: \blank{1.2cm} $\le x \le$ \blank{1.2cm} \quad
        Range: \blank{1.2cm} $\le y \le$ \blank{1.2cm}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Notation upgrade.} You will see the same two sets written with brackets:
$0 \le x \le 8$ is also written $[\,0, 8\,]$. A square bracket means the
endpoint \textbf{is} included; a round one means it is not. Either form is a
correct answer this year --- learn to read both.

\medskip
\textbf{From a formula.} Let $k(x) = \dfrac{8}{x + 3}$. At $x = -3$ the
denominator is \blank{1.2cm}, and division by zero is \blank{2.4cm} --- there
is no output at all. So the domain is every real number \textbf{except}
\blank{1.4cm}.
\end{notesbox}
