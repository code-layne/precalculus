    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will read a function's behavior from a pre-drawn
    graph: the intervals where it is increasing, decreasing, or constant, written as open
    intervals of $x$-values; its relative and absolute maximum and minimum, each given as a
    \textit{value} ($y$) and the input where it \textit{occurs} ($x$); and where it is concave
    up or concave down, with its points of inflection --- keeping the direction a graph goes
    separate from the way it bends.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topics 1.1 and 1.4 --- Skills 2.A, 3.A)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{AP Skill 2.A} --- Identify information from graphical, numerical, analytical, and
verbal representations to answer a question. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A function is \textbf{increasing} on an interval when larger inputs always give larger
        outputs, and \textbf{decreasing} when larger inputs always give smaller outputs ---
        read left to right, the graph rises or falls. (1.1.A.3, 1.1.A.4)
  \item An interval of increase or decrease is a stretch of \textbf{inputs}: it is written
        in $x$-values, as an open interval such as $(0, 2)$.
  \item A \textbf{relative maximum or minimum} occurs where the graph switches between
        increasing and decreasing; the greatest output anywhere (endpoints included) is the
        \textbf{absolute maximum}, the least the \textbf{absolute minimum}. (1.4.A.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function with varying levels of
precision. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A graph is \textbf{concave up} where it bends like a cup and \textbf{concave down}
        where it bends like a cap; read as rates, concave up means the rate of change is
        increasing and concave down that it is decreasing --- the language 2.6 makes
        precise. (1.1.B.3, 1.1.B.4)
  \item A \textbf{point of inflection} is where the concavity changes, up to down or down to
        up. (1.4.A.5)
  \item Direction and bend combine four ways: rising faster and faster, rising more and more
        slowly, falling faster and faster, falling more and more slowly.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{The distinction the lesson is built on.} &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item An extremum's \textbf{value} is a $y$-value; it \textbf{occurs at} an $x$-value. The
        point $(2, 600)$ says ``a maximum of 600 feet, at mile 2.''
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} students report with the wrong coordinate ---
        increasing intervals written in $y$-values (``increasing on $(300, 600)$''), or the
        $x$-location named as ``the maximum.'' Problems 11 and 12 of the notes put the two
        readings side by side and make students choose.
  \item \textbf{Second trap:} ``increasing'' is treated as ``concave up.'' Problem 13 finds an
        interval that is increasing and concave \emph{down}.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Increasing / decreasing & On an interval: as the inputs increase, the outputs always increase / always decrease. The graph rises / falls left to right. \\
Constant & On an interval: the outputs stay the same; the graph is flat. \\
Relative (local) maximum / minimum & A peak / a valley: an output greater / less than every nearby output, where the graph switches between increasing and decreasing. \\
Absolute (global) maximum / minimum & The greatest / least output anywhere on the graph, endpoints included. \\
Concave up / concave down & The graph bends upward like a cup / downward like a cap. \\
Point of inflection & A point where the graph's concavity changes, from up to down or from down to up. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review (5 min)]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: writing a set of numbers in interval notation, open and closed;
    reading a graph in both directions --- an output from an input, then the input that
    gives an output --- and stating domain and range in interval notation (2.4);
    finding the slope of a line through two points, and the sign of the slope of a line that
    falls from left to right --- the straight-line version of increasing and decreasing.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook (2 min)]{lilac}
    \textbf{Where was the top?} Two friends hiked the same trail and are describing the top of
    the first big climb.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item On the board: Ana says ``The top was \textit{at mile 2}.'' Ben says ``No --- the
              top was \textit{600 feet}.'' Ask: can they both be right?
        \item Push until someone says it: Ana told you \textbf{where} the top is, Ben told you
              \textbf{how high}. Both are describing one point, $(2, 600)$ --- one number from
              each axis.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} if a test asked ``what is the maximum?'', which friend gave the
    answer? Today every answer has to say whether it is a \textit{where} ($x$) or a
    \textit{how high} ($y$).
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- I Do / We Do / You Do]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{I DO --- Part 1: Which Way It Goes, and Where It Turns} (10 min)

\textit{Notes rows INCREASING \& DECREASING and MAXIMUM \& MINIMUM: read each definition, mark
up the trail graph, work problems 1 and 5. Teacher works; students watch and annotate. Nothing
is cold-called in this phase.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the first row's sentences aloud, then trace the trail with a finger, left to right,
        saying ``up\ldots\ up\ldots\ top\ldots\ down\ldots'' Fill the label box
        (\textit{decreasing}) yourself.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 1} aloud and narrate the axis: ``I'm asking \emph{where} it rises,
        so I read the mile markers --- $(0, 2)$ and $(6, 8)$.'' Write a wrong
        $(300, 600)$ beside it and cross it out.
  \item Read the MAXIMUM \& MINIMUM sentences. On the second copy of the trail, trace the two
        dashed guide lines: gold goes to the $y$-axis (how high), red to the $x$-axis (where).
        Fill both label boxes: \textit{600 ft} and \textit{2}.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 5} in a full sentence: ``a relative maximum of 600 feet, occurring
        at mile 2.'' Value first, then location, every time.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 2: Intervals and Extrema on the Trail} (7 min)

\textit{Problems 2--4 and 6--8. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 2} adds the context: downhill. \textbf{Problem 3} is why the intervals
        are open --- at the peak the trail is level for an instant, rising before and falling
        after. \textbf{Problem 4} names \textit{constant} on the battery graph.
  \item \textbf{Problem 7} is the surprise: the absolute maximum is 700 feet at the trail's
        \emph{end}, not the peak. Ask the class to compare every height, endpoints included.
        \textbf{Problem 8} names the valley as both relative and absolute.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 3: Concavity and the Point of Inflection} (6 min)

\textit{Notes row CONCAVITY. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one. Teacher
works problem 9; the class works the four label boxes and problem 10.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the definition: cup is up, cap is down. Then the four small curves --- the class
        labels each \textit{up} or \textit{down}, and you read its caption aloud so the
        sentence and the shape arrive together.
  \item Say plainly: \emph{two} of the four rising-or-falling curves are concave up --- one
        rises, one falls. Concavity does not tell you the direction.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 9} on the small trail: the cap over the peak, the cup under the
        valley, the switch at $d = 4$. \textbf{Problem 10} names the circled point
        $(4, 400)$ and what the trail is doing there.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 4: When the Readings Disagree} (9 min)

\textit{Notes row WHEN READINGS DISAGREE --- the crux. Class fills the blanks together;
cold-call every one. Teacher works problem 11; the class works 12--14.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Work \textbf{problem 11}: Sam read heights from the valley to the peak; the correct
        interval is $(1, 5)$. Name the axis out loud.
  \item \textbf{Problem 12 is the crux}: Maya says 1, Tavi says 4. Do not accept ``Tavi''
        alone --- make the class say what 1 \emph{is} (where the maximum occurs).
  \item \textbf{Problem 13} is the second trap: $(-1, 1)$ rises but bends like a cap.
        \textbf{Problem 14} is its mirror, falling but bending like a cup.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{YOU DO --- Part 5: One Day's Weather} (13 min)

\textit{Notes row ONE DAY'S WEATHER, problems 15--18 --- the whole independent phase. Students
work alone; circulate and say nothing a neighbor could say instead.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 15} reads direction in hours; \textbf{16} is the crux transferred ---
        four extrema, each a temperature \emph{and} an hour, and the ends (50 and 45) must be
        checked.
  \item \textbf{Problem 17} reads concavity and the inflection point $(10, 55)$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question: a forecaster mistakes ``rising'' for
        ``rising faster and faster'' --- the second trap, in words, with no partner to catch it.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the \textbf{You Do} phase --- the thirteen minutes where students are working
problems 15--18 without you --- and check. The crux is \textbf{problem 12} (the maximum is 4,
not the location 1); its You Do transfer is \textbf{problem 16}. The second trap is
\textbf{problem 13}; its transfer is \textbf{problem 18}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error (the target misconception):} intervals written in $y$-values ---
        ``$T$ is increasing on $(40, 70)$.'' Cold-call: ``Is 40 an hour or a temperature?
        Which axis do intervals live on?''
  \item \textbf{Common error (the target misconception):} the hour given as the maximum ---
        ``the maximum is 15.'' Ask: ``Fifteen \emph{what}? Degrees or hours?'' and have the
        student say the full sentence: value, then ``at,'' then location.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} the highest turning point taken as the absolute maximum without
        checking the endpoints. Point at the two end dots and ask for their heights.
  \item \textbf{Common error (the second trap):} ``rising, so concave up.'' Ask: ``Is it a cup
        or a cap?'' --- then point at the four small curves in the CONCAVITY row.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} closed brackets or the endpoints of a turn included in an
        interval of increase. Accept them without comment if the $x$-values are right; the
        coordinate is the point of the lesson.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Differentiation --- During You Do (13 min)]{redbox}
There is no separate activity sheet: students work the notes' You Do row (problems 15--18)
alone while you circulate. Differentiate by \textit{where you stand}, not by handing out
different paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Support.} Sit with a struggling student on \textbf{problem 15} and have them
        trace the curve left to right with a pencil, saying ``down, down, turn, up\ldots''
        and writing the \emph{hour} under each turn before writing any interval. The hour
        labels \emph{are} the endpoints of the intervals.
  \item \textbf{On level.} \textbf{Problem 16} is the crux transfer. Do not answer it; ask
        ``value or location?'' for each number they have written, check that they compared
        the two end heights, and walk away.
  \item \textbf{Extend.} \textbf{Problems 17 and 18} are the finisher's questions. 17 rewards a
        student who reads the bend separately from the direction across $(5, 15)$, where the
        curve rises the whole way but changes bend at $h = 10$. 18 asks them to overturn a
        plausible claim in words --- a student who adds that ``faster and faster'' was true on
        $(5, 10)$ has seen the whole picture. Either answer presents in the Debrief.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard[20]
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (5 min)]{redbox}
Whole class, teacher-led, packets still open. Three moves, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Share out the You Do} (3 min) --- one answer per problem, not a full review.
        Problem 15: the three intervals, in hours. Problem 16: ``a maximum of $70^\circ$ at
        hour 15'' --- said as a sentence, value first. Problem 17: the inflection point
        $(10, 55)$. Problem 18: ``rising, but more and more slowly.'' If a student wrote 18
        cleanly, they present it --- it is the only exposure the rest of the class gets
        before homework problems 7 and 8 ask for the same reading.
  \item \textbf{Name the headline} (1 min) --- say it, then make the class say it back:
        \textit{intervals and locations are $x$-values; a maximum or minimum is a $y$-value.
        Which way a graph goes and which way it bends are two different questions.} Point at
        the two dashed guide lines on the trail while you say it.
  \item \textbf{Point forward} (1 min) --- 2.6 stops asking \emph{which way} and starts
        measuring \emph{how fast}: rising more and more slowly will become a rate of change
        that is getting smaller.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Do not} re-teach concavity here, take new questions, or let the homework start early.
If the launch shows it did not land, it is the 2.6 warm-up's job, not this minute's.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Homework Launch (3 min)]{redbox}
\textbf{There is no exit ticket.} The formative read comes twice: circulating the You Do, and
again here. Write the due date on the board, have students copy it into the cover's Homework
row, then start \textbf{homework problems 2 and 4} alone and in silence (3 minutes). Circulate
straight to the students you flagged during You Do.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Problem 2} is the interval half of the misconception: a classmate wrote the
        drone's heights $(0, 12)$ as the interval. A student who agrees with the classmate is
        still reading the $y$-axis.
  \item \textbf{Problem 4} is the diagnostic: value and location for both turns, then an
        absolute minimum at the \emph{left endpoint}, $-3$ at $x = -3$, lower than the valley.
        A student who writes ``the relative max is $-1$'' has swapped the coordinates; one who
        gives $-2$ as the absolute minimum never checked the ends.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Sort what you see into three piles} as you walk: \textit{coordinates solid} (nothing to
do), \textit{value and location swapped} (a 2.6 warm-up item that asks for a sentence ``\ldots
of \_\_ at \_\_''), and \textit{endpoints ignored} (a one-minute check-in at the door of 2.6
with the trail graph's 700-foot end).
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- printed, graded (2 pages).} Last in the packet; due on the date the teacher
sets. Every context is new. \textit{Part A} (1--2) reads a drone's altitude: increasing,
constant, and decreasing intervals in seconds, then a classmate's $y$-value interval to fix.
\textit{Part B} (3--4) reads a new graph $p$: intervals, both turning points as value and
location, and an absolute minimum that sits at an endpoint. \textit{Part C} (5--7) is
concavity: matching four curve shapes to their descriptions, $p$'s concavity and inflection
point, and app downloads that are increasing and concave down, in words. \textit{Part D} (8--9)
refutes ``concave up, so increasing'' on $p$, and problem 9 asks for the headline in writing.

\textbf{AP Practice --- extra credit (2 pages).} Optional, before the homework in the packet.
Section I is five AP-style multiple-choice items (four options) whose distractors are the
lesson's errors: the value and its location swapped (1C), concave down read as decreasing or
increasing read as concave up (2A, 2B, 2D), an interval read in $y$-values (3B), a turning point
called an inflection point (4A, 4B), and the $x$-location or the relative maximum given as the
absolute maximum value (5A, 5B, 5C). Section II is one free-response question on a harbor's tide
graph: increasing and decreasing intervals (a), the absolute maximum in context (b), a worker who
gives the hour as ``the low tide'' (c), direction and concavity together on $(3, 6)$ (d), and when
the water falls fastest (e) --- the finisher's question, and a preview of 2.6.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (2.6, Average Rate of Change, Secant Lines, and the Difference
Quotient) measures \emph{how fast} a function changes over an interval. The homework's closing
box asks how many meters per second the drone climbs; 2.6 calls that the average rate of change,
and re-reads today's concavity as a rate of change that is itself increasing or decreasing.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1 matters more than it looks: every answer in
today's notes is an open interval, and a student who cannot write $(2, 5)$ for $2 < x < 5$ will
spend the I Do on notation instead of on the graph. Item 2 reads one graph in both directions on
purpose --- (a) goes from $x$ to $y$, (b) from $y$ back to $x$ --- which is the exact move the
lesson's misconception gets wrong; a student who answers (b) with a $y$-value is a student to
watch at problem 12. Items 3--4 are slope and its sign, the straight-line version of rising and
falling, and 2.6 builds directly on them.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The notes are one Main Ideas / Notes table, four instruction rows and a You Do row; in each
instruction row you read the printed definition, mark up the display, and work the first problem
of the grid (1, 5, 9, 11), and the class works the rest with the pen in their hand, every problem
cold-called. The trail is the spine of the first three rows and is drawn three times on purpose
--- once plain, once with the two guide lines, once small beside the concavity shapes --- so no
student flips pages to read it. Narrate the axis every time you read a number: ``where'' is the
mile marker, ``how high'' is the feet. That habit is what problems 11--12 test.

Problem 7 is worth slowing down for: the absolute maximum is the trail's end, 700 feet, not the
600-foot peak. Students who learned ``the top of the hill is the maximum'' will resist; make them
compare every height on the graph, endpoints included. Relative extrema are read at the turning
points only --- if a student asks whether the end at mile 8 is a relative maximum too, say that
in this course a relative extremum is a turn, and the endpoints compete only for absolute.

WHEN READINGS DISAGREE is the lesson. Problem 12 asks for the reason, not the name: ``Tavi'' is
half an answer; ``the maximum is 4, and it occurs at $x = 1$'' is the whole one. Problems 13--14
break ``increasing means concave up'' by finding the other two combinations on one graph. The
You Do (15--18) is the entire independent phase and carries thirteen minutes; problem 16 is the
crux with a new context and four extrema to report, and problem 18 is the second trap in a
forecaster's words. If the clock slips, cut problem 14 and let the Debrief carry it.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Debrief]
Five minutes, and on this lesson they are at risk --- four readings share the guided phase and it
will try to borrow them. Borrow from problem 4 (the battery) or problem 14 instead; both have
twins in the homework (problems 1 and 8). Do not borrow from the three-minute Homework Launch
either: with no exit ticket, it is the last look you get before the work leaves the room. The
share-out is one answer per You Do problem, said as a sentence, so that a student who stalled on
15 still hears ``a maximum of $70^\circ$ at hour 15'' --- the exact shape of homework problem 4,
which they will otherwise meet alone at home.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
Extra credit, optional, and not a substitute for the homework --- say so when you hand out the
packet. Score it however extra credit works in your gradebook; the cover gives it a $+$ cell
outside the total. Multiple-choice answers are B, C, A, C, D. Item 5 is the most useful one to
glance at: a student who circles (B) 6 or (C) 0 gave an $x$-location as the maximum value --- the
lesson's misconception exactly --- and one who circles (A) 3 stopped at the peak and never checked
the endpoint. On item 2, (A) is the second trap (concave down read as ``faster''). In the free
response, accept $(3, 9)$ or $[3, 9]$ in (a); part (c) earns credit only if it names 9 as a
\emph{time} and gives the low tide as 2 feet. Part (e) is the deepest question on the page ---
credit any answer that places the fastest fall at $t = 6$ because the bend switches there.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Graded, two pages, due on the date you set; students start problems 2 and 4 in the Homework
Launch. Grade problems 4 and 8 for accuracy and the rest for completion and shown work if time is
short: 4 (value vs.\ location, and an endpoint that beats the valley) and 8 (concave up but
decreasing) are the two items that say whether the crux and the second trap landed. On problem 1,
$(0, 12)$ for the increasing interval is the misconception again --- if it is common, reuse
problem 11 of the notes in the 2.6 warm-up. Problem 7 is the one that predicts 2.6: a student who
can say ``still growing, but more slowly'' is ready to read concavity as a rate of change that is
getting smaller; one who writes ``downloads are going down'' has merged the two questions.
\end{teachernote}
