    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will describe the end behavior of a
    polynomial function in limit notation, explain \textit{why} the leading term
    decides that behavior --- because for large $|x|$ every lower-degree term is
    negligible beside it --- and read end behavior off a polynomial written in
    standard form or in factored form.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topic 1.6 --- Skill 3.A)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{AP Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function with varying levels
of precision, depending on the function representation and the mathematical
tools available. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item As the input values of a nonconstant polynomial increase without bound,
        the output values either increase or decrease without bound:
        $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$ or $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) =
        -\infty$. (1.6.A.1)
  \item The same is true as input values decrease without bound:
        $\lim_{x \to -\infty} p(x) = \infty$ or $= -\infty$. (1.6.A.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{LO 1.6.A} --- Describe end behaviors of polynomial functions. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item The \textbf{degree} and the \textbf{sign of the leading term} determine
        end behavior, \textit{because} for inputs of large magnitude the
        leading term dominates the values of every lower-degree term.
        (1.6.A.3)
  \item Even degree $\Rightarrow$ both ends run the same way; odd degree
        $\Rightarrow$ the ends run opposite ways. A negative leading
        coefficient flips both.
  \item Limit notation is \textit{language for a described behavior}, not a
        computation --- and it is the language calculus will use.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
End behavior            & What the output values of $p$ do as the input runs off toward $\infty$ and toward $-\infty$ --- the two far ends of the graph. \\
Increase without bound  & The outputs pass every number you can name and never turn back. ``Decrease without bound'' is the same idea downward. \\
Limit notation          & $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$, read ``as $x$ increases without bound, $p(x)$ increases without bound.'' A shorthand for a description, not an arithmetic answer. \\
Leading term            & The term of highest degree, written $a_n x^n$. In factored form it is the product of the leading pieces of all the factors. \\
Leading coefficient     & The number $a_n$ multiplying the leading term. Only its \textit{sign} matters for end behavior. \\
Dominance               & For large $|x|$, the leading term is so much larger than the rest that the rest is negligible: the ratio (rest)~$\div$~(leading term) shrinks toward $0$. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: totaling the degree of a polynomial from its factored form
    and separating real zeros from non-real ones (Lesson 2.4), and two Algebra 2
    prerequisites this lesson depends on --- naming the leading term and leading
    coefficient of a polynomial whose terms are \textit{out of degree order},
    and evaluating a polynomial at a large input without a calculator. Item 3
    is the numerical seed of the whole lesson: the cubic term has already
    overtaken the quadratic one by $x = 100$.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{lilac}
    \textbf{The graph that lies.} Project the pre-drawn graph of
    $p(x) = x^3 - 100x^2$ from the notes. On the window $-20 \le x \le 70$ the
    curve heads down and flattens out near the bottom --- it looks for all the
    world like a function that has given up and stays negative.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Ask for a prediction: what is $p(1000)$ --- big and negative, or
              something else?
        \item Then give it: $p(1000) = 900{,}000{,}000$. Positive, and nearly a
              billion.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} Nothing about the window was wrong; the window
    was just too small. $-100x^2$ is winning at $x = 20$ and losing badly at
    $x = 1000$. Today we find out which term is in charge \textit{eventually},
    and we learn the notation for saying so.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{Part 1: Limit Notation as Vocabulary} (10 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Say the words first, symbols second: ``as $x$ increases without bound,
        $p(x)$ increases without bound.'' Then write
        $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$ underneath it.
  \item Insist on the reading: this is a \textbf{description}, not a
        computation. $\infty$ is not a number you plugged in and not a number
        that came out --- it is shorthand for ``and it never stops.''
  \item Four sentences, four symbols. Drill the translation both ways: give a
        sentence, get the notation; give the notation, get the sentence.
  \item Flag the payoff: this is the notation calculus runs on. Getting it
        fluent now costs one lesson and saves a semester.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 2: Why the Leading Term Wins} (14 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item This is the conceptual heart --- EK 1.6.A.3 is a \textit{because}, and
        the table is the evidence for it. Do not skip to the rules.
  \item Build the dominance table for $p(x) = x^3 - 100x^2$ at
        $x = 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000$: leading term, the rest, $p(x)$, and the
        ratio (rest) $\div$ (leading term).
  \item That last column is the whole argument. It equals $-100/x$, so it runs
        $-10, -2, -1, -0.2, -0.1$ --- the rest becomes a rounding error beside
        the leading term.
  \item Land it: end behavior of $x^3 - 100x^2$ is the end behavior of $x^3$.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{Part 3: The Four Cases} (14 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item Only two things about the leading term matter: is the degree
        \textbf{even or odd}, and is the coefficient \textbf{positive or
        negative}. Two choices twice over gives four cases and no more.
  \item Fill the $2 \times 2$ table together, one row at a time, tying each row
        to its pre-drawn graph on the same page.
  \item The one-sentence summary to write down: even degree sends both ends
        the same way, odd degree sends them opposite ways, and a negative
        leading coefficient flips both ends.
  \item Sanity check to teach: an odd-degree polynomial has one end up and one
        end down, so it must cross the $x$-axis --- exactly what 2.4 proved
        from the zero count.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Part 4: Reading It Off Any Form} (12 min)

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Standard form} hands you the leading term for free --- as long
        as the terms are in degree order. Scramble one on purpose
        ($9 + 2x^4 - x^7$) so nobody grabs the first term they see.
  \item \textbf{Factored form} hides it. Add the exponents for the degree;
        multiply the leading pieces of the factors for the coefficient. Never
        expand.
  \item Work $q(x) = 2(x-3)^2(x+1)(x^2+4)$: degree $2+1+2 = 5$, leading term
        $2x^5$, so odd and positive.
  \item Close the arc: 2.4 counted the zeros --- the middle of the graph. 2.5
        describes the two far ends. Both are now on the table.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
While students work on guided notes and practice, circulate and check:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} taking the \textit{first} term written as the
        leading term. Cold-call on $p(x) = 9 + 2x^4 - x^7$: ``Which exponent is
        the biggest? Where is that term sitting?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} reading ``negative leading coefficient'' as
        ``all outputs are negative.'' Push back with the hook: $x^3 - 100x^2$
        is negative on a wide stretch and still runs to $+\infty$.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} in factored form, calling $(x^2+4)$ degree 1, or
        forgetting the outside constant when building the leading coefficient.
  \item \textbf{Notation:} require $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$, not
        ``goes to infinity'' and not $p(\infty) = \infty$. The second is not
        notation this course allows --- $\infty$ is never an input.
  \item \textbf{Language:} ``increases without bound,'' not ``gets really
        big.'' The phrase is the definition.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
Students work on the \textbf{Group Activity: The End Behavior Desk} in leveled
tiers. Tier E reads two pre-drawn graphs --- no tier draws anything.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tier R (Remediate):} The leading term is supplied for each of
        four polynomials; students classify degree parity and coefficient sign,
        then read both limit statements straight off the four-case table.
  \item \textbf{Tier A (Approaching Proficiency):} Students extract the leading
        term themselves --- two in standard form (one scrambled), two in
        factored form --- and write both limit statements for each.
  \item \textbf{Tier E (Extension):} Students read end behavior off two
        pre-drawn graphs and write it in limit notation, run the dominance
        ratio for $x^4 - 500x^3$ to find where the leading term takes over,
        build a polynomial to a prescribed end behavior and zero count, and
        justify in writing why two polynomials with the same leading term must
        share end behavior (AP Skill 3.A).
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (last 5--7 minutes, individual, collected):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For $p(x) = -2x^5 + 7x^3 - x + 3$: name the leading term, then write
        both limit statements.
  \item For $q(x) = 3(x-1)^2(x+4)^2$ in factored form (worked space provided):
        give the degree and leading coefficient, then both limit statements.
  \item Reverse it --- a polynomial has $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$ and
        $\lim_{x \to -\infty} p(x) = -\infty$. Is the degree even or odd? Is
        the leading coefficient positive or negative?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Assessment note:} Item 2 is the diagnostic. A student who expands
$3(x-1)^2(x+4)^2$ before answering has the right answer and the wrong method,
and will run out of time on a real assessment; a student who writes degree 2 has
not seen that the exponents add. Item 3 tests whether the four-case table was
memorized as a table or understood as two independent yes/no questions.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} 5 problems covering end behavior from standard form (one
deliberately scrambled), end behavior from factored form with a worked degree
and leading coefficient, a dominance-ratio table for $x^4 - 1000x^3$, reading
end behavior off two pre-drawn graphs, and one AP-style multiple-choice item.

\textbf{Extension (optional):} Factor the leading term out of
$p(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + a_0$ to get
$p(x) = a_n x^n\!\left(1 + \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n x} + \dots +
\frac{a_0}{a_n x^n}\right)$. Every fraction in the bracket has an $x$ in the
denominator, so the bracket closes on $1$ --- which is the dominance table of
Part 2, written once and for all.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (2.6, Equivalent Representations of Polynomial
Expressions) puts the two halves side by side: the \textit{standard} form is
the one that hands you the end behavior, the \textit{factored} form is the one
that hands you the zeros, and they are the same polynomial.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 2 is the one to watch --- the
terms are out of degree order on purpose, and a student who answers ``$7$'' or
``$x^2$'' has revealed exactly the habit Part 4 has to break. Do not correct it
at the desk; collect the wrong answers and open Part 4 with them. Item 3 is
arithmetic students can do on paper: $100^3 = 1{,}000{,}000$ and
$50 \cdot 100^2 = 500{,}000$. If more than a third of the room stalls there, the
dominance table in Part 2 needs to be worked column by column at the board
rather than in pairs.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
Part 2 is the centerpiece; protect its fourteen minutes. The temptation is to
state the four cases early and let the table become decoration --- resist it,
because the four cases are memorizable in ninety seconds and the reason behind
them is the only part that survives to calculus. Work the $x = 10$ row aloud,
let pairs do $x = 50$ and $x = 100$, then work $x = 500$ and $x = 1000$ together
and make somebody say out loud what the last column is doing. Part 1 can be
brisk but must not be skipped: the notation is assessed on every item today. If
time is short, compress Part 3 --- the graphs carry it --- never Parts 1 or 2.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
Tier R exists to make the four-case lookup automatic; if a group finishes it in
four minutes, that is the design working, and they move to Tier A. Tier A's
item 1(b) is the scrambled polynomial and item 2 is factored form --- those two
are the whole point of the tier, so check them before checking anything else.
In Tier E, question 2 asks where the leading term overtakes the rest for
$x^4 - 500x^3$; the answer is $x = 500$, and students who find it by testing
values rather than by solving are doing exactly the right thing. Question 4 is
the writing task; accept any version of ``the lower-degree terms stop mattering,
so only the leading term is left to decide the ends.''
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Collected, completion credit only. Sort by item 2 first: degree 4 and leading
coefficient 3 is the target; ``degree 2'' is the reteach pile and needs sixty
seconds on adding exponents at the start of 2.6. Item 3 is the fastest read on
conceptual understanding --- a student who has to reconstruct the whole table to
answer it has memorized rather than understood, which is fine for today and
worth a revisit before the unit test. Accept ``odd'' and ``positive'' without
justification.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Problem 3's ratio table is the one to grade first; it isolates dominance from
every other skill and a wrong final row means Part 2 did not land. Problem 5 is
the AP-style item --- distractor B ($-2x^4 + x$) is chosen by students who see
the negative sign, stop, and never check the degree's parity, and it is worth
two minutes tomorrow because Unit 2's test will ask it again. The extension is
the algebra behind Part 2; if anyone asked ``but does it \textit{always} win?''
during the lesson, assign it by name and open 2.6 with that student at the
board.
\end{teachernote}
