\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Limit Notation --- Language for the Two Far Ends}
Lesson 2.4 was about the middle of the graph, where the zeros live. This lesson
is about the two \textbf{far ends}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item If the input values of a nonconstant polynomial increase without bound,
        the output values must either increase without bound or
        \blank{2.8cm} without bound. There is no third option --- a polynomial
        cannot level off.
  \item In symbols: \quad $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$ \quad or \quad
        $\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = -\infty$.
  \item Going the other way, ``as the input values \textbf{decrease} without
        bound'' is written $x \to $ \blank{1.6cm}.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Notation} & \textbf{Said out loud} \\
\hline
$\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = \infty$ & As $x$ increases without bound, $p(x)$ increases without bound. \\
\hline
$\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = -\infty$ & \blank{7cm} \\
\hline
$\lim_{x \to -\infty} p(x) = \infty$ & \blank{7cm} \\
\hline
\blank{3.4cm} & As $x$ decreases without bound, $p(x)$ decreases without bound. \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\smallskip

\textbf{Careful.} $\infty$ is not a number. You never substitute it and you
never get it back as an output. The whole statement is a \blank{2.6cm} of a
behavior, not the answer to a computation --- so never write
$p(\infty) = \infty$. This is the notation calculus runs on, so it is worth
getting exactly right now.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{2. Why the Leading Term Wins}
\textbf{The claim.} The degree and the sign of the leading term determine end
behavior --- \textit{because} once the inputs are large, the leading term
dominates the values of every lower-degree term. That ``because'' needs
evidence, so here it is.

\smallskip
Let $p(x) = x^3 - 100x^2$. The leading term is \blank{1.6cm}, and ``the rest''
is $-100x^2$. Complete the last two columns.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{|r|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & \textbf{leading: $x^3$} & \textbf{the rest: $-100x^2$} & $p(x)$ & \textbf{rest $\div$ leading} \\
\hline
$10$      & $1 \times 10^{3}$    & $-1 \times 10^{4}$   & $-9 \times 10^{3}$ & $-10$ \\
\hline
$50$      & $1.25 \times 10^{5}$ & $-2.5 \times 10^{5}$ & \blank{1.9cm} & \blank{1.3cm} \\
\hline
$100$     & $1 \times 10^{6}$    & $-1 \times 10^{6}$   & \blank{1.9cm} & \blank{1.3cm} \\
\hline
$500$     & $1.25 \times 10^{8}$ & $-2.5 \times 10^{7}$ & \blank{1.9cm} & \blank{1.3cm} \\
\hline
$1{,}000$ & $1 \times 10^{9}$    & $-1 \times 10^{8}$   & \blank{1.9cm} & \blank{1.3cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\smallskip

The last column is the whole argument. Algebraically it is
$\dfrac{-100x^2}{x^3} = \dfrac{-100}{x}$, which shrinks toward \blank{1.2cm} as
$x$ increases. The rest of the polynomial does not become small --- at
$x = 1000$ it is a hundred million --- it becomes small \textit{compared with
the leading term}.

\medskip
\textbf{Conclusion (EK 1.6.A.3).} At the far ends, $x^3 - 100x^2$ behaves
exactly like \blank{1.6cm}. Every lower-degree term is negligible, so only the
\blank{2.6cm} term decides end behavior.

\medskip
\textbf{Back to the hook.} The window stopped at $x = 70$, and $-100x^2$ was
still ahead of $x^3$ there. It loses the lead for good at $x = $
\blank{1.4cm}, which the table above shows exactly.
\end{notesbox}

\textbf{One sentence to remember.} Even degree sends both ends the
\blank{1.8cm} way; odd degree sends them \blank{2.2cm} ways; and a negative
leading coefficient \blank{1.6cm} both ends.

\medskip
\textbf{A check on Lesson 2.4.} An odd-degree polynomial has one end up and one
end down, so its graph must cross the $x$-axis --- it must have at least one
\blank{1.6cm} zero. That is the same conclusion 2.4 reached by counting
conjugate pairs, arrived at from the opposite direction.

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Reading End Behavior Off Any Form}
\textbf{Standard form} hands you the leading term --- provided you read the
\textit{exponents} and not the left-to-right order.

\medskip
\textbf{Example 1.} $p(x) = 9 + 2x^4 - x^7$.

\smallskip
Leading term: \blank{2cm} \qquad Degree: \blank{1.2cm}, which is
\blank{1.4cm} \qquad Sign of $a_n$: \blank{1.6cm}

\smallskip
$\lim_{x \to \infty} p(x) = $ \blank{1.8cm} \qquad
$\lim_{x \to -\infty} p(x) = $ \blank{1.8cm}

\medskip
\textbf{Factored form} hides the leading term --- but you never have to expand.
Add the exponents to get the degree; multiply the leading pieces of the factors
to get the leading term.

\medskip
\textbf{Example 2.} $q(x) = 2(x-3)^2(x+1)(x^2+4)$.

\begin{work}
  \text{degree of } q &= 2 + 1 + 2 \\
                      &= 5 \\
  \text{leading term} &= 2 \cdot x^{2} \cdot x \cdot x^{2} \\
                      &= 2x^{5}
\end{work}

Odd degree, positive leading coefficient, so
$\lim_{x \to \infty} q(x) = $ \blank{1.8cm} and
$\lim_{x \to -\infty} q(x) = $ \blank{1.8cm}.

\medskip
\textbf{Two forms, two jobs.} Factored form told you the \blank{1.6cm} back in
Lesson 2.4; standard form tells you the \blank{2.6cm} today. Lesson 2.6 puts
them side by side and shows they are the same polynomial.
\end{notesbox}
