\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item I can read from a graph the intervals where a function is \textbf{increasing},
        \textbf{decreasing}, or \textbf{constant}, and write them as open intervals of
        $x$-values.
  \item I can find the \textbf{relative} and \textbf{absolute maximum and minimum} of a
        function from its graph, giving each extremum's \emph{value} (a $y$-value) and
        \emph{where} it occurs (an $x$-value).
  \item I can read where a graph is \textbf{concave up} or \textbf{concave down}, locate its
        \textbf{points of inflection}, and describe the four combinations --- such as
        ``rising, but more and more slowly.''
  \item I can tell apart two readings that disagree: a value from its location, and
        increasing from concave up.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small
A graph answers two different questions. \textbf{Which way does it go?} Rising is
\textbf{increasing}, falling is \textbf{decreasing} --- and the turns between them are the
\textbf{maximum} and \textbf{minimum} points. \textbf{Which way does it bend?} Like a cup is
\textbf{concave up}, like a cap is \textbf{concave down}, and a \textbf{point of inflection} is
where the bend switches. Every interval and location lives on the $x$-axis; every maximum or
minimum \emph{value} lives on the $y$-axis.
\end{remindbox}
