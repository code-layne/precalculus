\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item I can find the \textbf{average rate of change} of a function on an interval from a
        table, a graph, or a formula, and state it with units in a context.
  \item I can say what the sign of an average rate of change tells me about how two quantities
        change together --- increasing or decreasing.
  \item I can explain the average rate of change as the slope of a \textbf{secant line} and
        write that line's equation in point-slope form.
  \item I can compare average rates over equal intervals to decide whether a function is linear,
        concave up, or concave down.
  \item I can build $f(x + h)$ and simplify the \textbf{difference quotient} for a linear or a
        simple quadratic function, and check it against a numerical average rate of change.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small
An \textbf{average rate of change} is change in output over change in input --- the one
steady rate that would cover the same change over the same interval. On a graph it is the
slope of the \textbf{secant line} joining two points. Over equal intervals, the pattern of these
rates shows whether a function is linear or curving up or down. The \textbf{difference
quotient} is the same ratio on the interval from $x$ to $x + h$ --- and $f(x + h)$ means the
rule run on the input $x + h$, not $f(x)$ with $h$ added on.
\end{remindbox}
