\begin{teachernote}
Item 1 checks that students can compute differences and ratios; expect some to stop after
first differences without checking ratios. Item 2 reinforces that exponential functions
eventually outgrow linear. Item 3 is a direct recall of the ratio-signal rule from Lesson 2.5.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=14pt]

\item The table below shows values of a function. Compute the first differences ($\Delta_1$),
      the second differences ($\Delta_2$), and the successive ratios ($r$).

      \vspace{0.06in}
      \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
      \begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c}
      $x$ & $y$ & $\Delta_1 = y_{\text{next}} - y$ & Ratio $= y_{\text{next}} / y$ \\ \hline
      0 & 4   & \ans{8}  & \ans{3} \\
      1 & 12  & \ans{24} & \ans{3} \\
      2 & 36  & \ans{72} & \ans{3} \\
      3 & 108 & --- & --- \\
      \end{tabular}

      \vspace{0.04in}
      Based on your table, this data is best modeled by a(n) \ans{exponential} function.

\item Consider $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = 2^x$.

      \vspace{0.04in}
      $f(5) = $ \ans{11} \hspace{1cm} $g(5) = $ \ans{32}

      \vspace{0.04in}
      Which function grows faster? \ans{$g(x) = 2^x$ (exponential)}

\item A data set shows that when the input increases by 1, the output is multiplied by
      approximately 1.5 each time. What type of model fits this data best?

      \vspace{0.04in}
      \ans{Exponential} \quad How do you know? \ans{Constant successive ratios signal exponential growth.}

\end{enumerate}
