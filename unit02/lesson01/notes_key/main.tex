\begin{objectivebox}
By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\ldots
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Read the vertex, axis of symmetry, and zeros of a quadratic from its
        \ans{standard}, \ans{vertex}, and \ans{factored} forms.
  \item Compute the \ans{average rate of change} of a quadratic over an
        interval and recognize it as the slope of a \ans{secant} line.
  \item Use \ans{first} and \ans{second} differences to tell a linear
        function from a quadratic one and to justify \ans{concavity}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\begin{vocabbox}
Fill in each term as we define it together.
\par\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{\textcolor{plum}{Vertex form:}}\\[1pt]
\ansline{$f(x) = a(x-h)^2 + k$ --- the vertex is $(h,k)$, on the axis of symmetry $x = h$.}\\[3pt]
\noindent\textbf{\textcolor{plum}{Average rate of change (AROC):}}\\[1pt]
\ansline{Over $[a,b]$: the ratio $(f(b)-f(a))/(b-a)$ --- output change divided by input change.}\\[3pt]
\noindent\textbf{\textcolor{plum}{Secant line:}}\\[1pt]
\ansline{The line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$; its slope is the AROC over $[a,b]$.}\\[3pt]
\noindent\textbf{\textcolor{plum}{First differences:}}\\[1pt]
\ansline{Successive output differences for equally spaced inputs; with step 1 they are the AROCs.}\\[3pt]
\noindent\textbf{\textcolor{plum}{Second differences:}}\\[1pt]
\ansline{The differences of the first differences --- constant exactly when the function is quadratic.}\\[3pt]
\noindent\textbf{\textcolor{plum}{Concave up / concave down:}}\\[1pt]
\ansline{Up when the AROCs increase, down when they decrease.}
\end{vocabbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Three Forms, Three Features}
A quadratic can be written three different ways. They are the same function
--- but each one hands you a different feature for free.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Form} & \textbf{Looks like} & \textbf{Shows you at a glance} \\
\hline
Standard & $ax^2 + bx + c$ & the $y$-intercept, which is \ans{$c$} \\
\hline
Vertex   & $a(x-h)^2 + k$  & the vertex, the point ( \ans{$h$} , \ans{$k$} ) \\
\hline
Factored & $a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$ & the \ans{zeros} : $r_1$ and $r_2$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\smallskip

From standard form, the \textbf{axis of symmetry} is the vertical line
$x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, and the vertex sits on it.

\smallskip
\textbf{Example 1.} Let $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5$. Find the vertex.

\begin{work}
  x &= -\frac{b}{2a} \\
    &= -\frac{-6}{2(1)} \\
    &= 3 \\
  f(3) &= (3)^2 - 6(3) + 5 \\
       &= -4
\end{work}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item Vertex: ( \ans{3} , \ans{$-4$} ) \qquad Axis of symmetry:
        $x = $ \ans{3}
  \item Vertex form: $f(x) = $ \ans{$(x-3)^2 - 4$}
  \item Factored form: $f(x) = (x-1)(x-5)$, so the zeros are \ans{1} and
        \ans{5}
  \item Here $a = 1 > 0$, so the parabola opens \ans{upward} and the graph
        is concave \ans{up}.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{2. Average Rate of Change --- The Secant Line Returns}
From Lesson 1.3: the \textbf{average rate of change} of $f$ over $[a, b]$ is

\begin{center}
$\text{AROC over } [a,b] \;=\;
\dfrac{\text{change in \ans{output}}}{\text{change in \ans{input}}}
\;=\; \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$
\end{center}

and it is the \ans{slope} of the \textbf{secant line} through $(a, f(a))$
and $(b, f(b))$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Example 2.} For the same $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5$, find the AROC over
$[1, 4]$.

\begin{work}
  f(1) &= (1)^2 - 6(1) + 5 \\
       &= 0 \\
  f(4) &= (4)^2 - 6(4) + 5 \\
       &= -3 \\
  \text{AROC} &= \frac{-3 - 0}{4 - 1} \\
              &= -1
\end{work}

The dashed line below is that secant. Its slope is the AROC.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.72cm, y=0.32cm]
  \draw[linegray, very thin, step=1] (0,-5) grid (6,5);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (6.6,0) node[below right] {\small $x$};
  \draw[->, thick] (0,-5) -- (0,5.6) node[above] {\small $f(x)$};
  \foreach \x in {1,2,3,4,5,6} \node[below] at (\x,0) {\small \x};
  \foreach \y in {-4,-2,2,4} \node[left] at (0,\y) {\small \y};
  \draw[very thick, plum] plot [smooth] coordinates
    {(0,5) (1,0) (2,-3) (3,-4) (4,-3) (5,0) (6,5)};
  \draw[dashed, thick, goldacc] (1,0) -- (4,-3);
  \fill[plum] (1,0) circle (2.5pt);
  \fill[plum] (3,-4) circle (2.5pt);
  \fill[plum] (4,-3) circle (2.5pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item AROC over $[3, 6]$: \quad $\dfrac{5 - (-4)}{6 - 3} = $ \ans{3}
  \item Same function, different interval, \ans{a different} rate. A line could
        never do that --- and that difference is the rest of the lesson.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{3. Difference Tables --- Linear vs.\ Quadratic}
When the inputs are equally spaced, a \textbf{first difference} is one output
minus the one before it, and a \textbf{second difference} is one first
difference minus the one before it. With a step of $1$, each first difference
\textit{is} an AROC.

\medskip
\textbf{Linear:} $L(x) = 3x - 2$

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
\hline
$L(x)$ & \ans{$-2$} & \ans{1} & \ans{4} & \ans{7} & \ans{10} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\smallskip

First differences: \ans{3} , \ans{3} , \ans{3} , \ans{3}

Second differences: \ans{0} , \ans{0} , \ans{0}

For a \textbf{linear} function the AROC is \ans{constant} over every interval,
so the second differences are all \ans{0}.

\medskip
\textbf{Quadratic:} $Q(x) = 2x^2 - 4x + 1$

\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
\hline
$Q(x)$ & 1 & $-1$ & 1 & 7 & 17 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\smallskip

First differences: \ans{$-2$} , \ans{2} , \ans{6} , \ans{10}

Second differences: \ans{4} , \ans{4} , \ans{4}

The first differences are \textbf{not} constant --- but look at how they
change: each one is \ans{4} more than the one before. The AROCs of a
quadratic form a \ans{linear} pattern of their own.

\medskip
\textbf{The headline:} for a quadratic, the average rates of change over
consecutive equal-length intervals change at a \ans{constant} rate.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.1in}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Second Differences, the Leading Coefficient, and Concavity}
\textbf{The fingerprint.} If the inputs step by $h$, then a quadratic
$f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ has constant second differences equal to $2ah^2$.
When the step is $h = 1$, that constant is simply $2a$.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=4pt]
  \item Check it on $Q(x) = 2x^2 - 4x + 1$: the second difference was
        \ans{4} , and $2a = 2(2) = $ \ans{4} --- they match.
  \item So a table alone gives you $a$: if the second differences are a
        constant $10$ with unit steps, then $a = $ \ans{5} .
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Concavity, from the numbers alone.} When the AROCs (first
differences) are \textit{increasing}, the graph is concave \ans{up};
when they are \textit{decreasing}, the graph is concave \ans{down}.

\smallskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Function} & \textbf{First differences} & \textbf{Second differences} \\
\hline
Linear    & constant                    & all \ans{zero} \\
\hline
Quadratic & change by a \ans{constant} step & constant and \ans{not zero} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\smallskip

Since $Q$'s first differences $-2, 2, 6, 10$ are increasing, $Q$ is concave
\ans{up} --- and $a = 2$ is \ans{positive} , which agrees.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=8pt]
  \item Let $p(x) = -x^2 + 6x$. Complete the table and the difference rows.

        \smallskip
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        $x$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
        \hline
        $p(x)$ & \ans{0} & \ans{5} & \ans{8} & \ans{9} & \ans{8} \\
        \hline
        \end{tabular}
        \smallskip

        First differences: \ans{5} , \ans{3} , \ans{1} ,
        \ans{$-1$}

        Second differences: \ans{$-2$} , \ans{$-2$} , \ans{$-2$}

        The graph is concave \ans{down} , and $a = $ \ans{$-1$} .

  \item A quadratic's AROCs over $[0,1]$, $[1,2]$, and $[2,3]$ are
        $1,\ 7,\ 13$.

        \begin{work}
          \text{step} &= 7 - 1 \\
                      &= 6 \\
          2a &= 6 \\
           a &= 3
        \end{work}

        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), itemsep=4pt]
          \item The AROCs step by \ans{6} each time, so the leading
                coefficient is $a = $ \ans{3} .
          \item The AROCs are increasing, so the graph is concave
                \ans{up} .
        \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}
