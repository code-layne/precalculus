\textbf{This is the densest period in the unit, on purpose.} Two topics --- complex numbers
and the parent-function library --- plus absolute value as a piecewise function share one
block, so the guided phase runs 23 minutes and the You Do takes 15, near the low end of its range.
The budget inside We Do is 6 minutes of complex practice, 9 for the library, 8 for absolute
value. \textbf{Protected:} the ABSOLUTE VALUE row (problems 11--14, the crux) and the You Do's
15 minutes --- 2.2 transforms $|x|$ first, problem 18 carries the piecewise habit, and with no
exit ticket, circulating the You Do is the period's only formative read; never cut it below 14.
\textbf{If the clock slips, cut in this order:} problem 10 (even vs.\ odd degree --- state
the rule aloud; the DeltaMath parent-function topic returns to it), then problem 3 (powers of
$i$ --- work one power on the board), then let the class fill gallery panels F--J by
themselves during the You Do's last minute rather than cold-calling each one.

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{AP Skill 1.B} --- Express expressions in equivalent forms: rewrite $\sqrt{-n}$ with
$i$, and simplify complex sums and products to $a + bi$. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item $i = \sqrt{-1}$, so $i^2 = -1$; $\sqrt{-n} = i\sqrt{n}$ for $n > 0$. The powers of $i$
        cycle $i, -1, -i, 1$.
  \item Rewrite with $i$ \textit{first}: $\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = -6$, not
        $\sqrt{36}$.
  \item Multiply like binomials, then replace $i^2$ with $-1$. A number times its conjugate is
        real: $(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2$.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 2.A} --- Identify information from graphical and analytical representations:
the ten parent functions. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Each parent function has a name, an equation, a shape, and key points with small
        whole-number coordinates; match any one to the other three.
  \item Domain is read left to right, range bottom to top. $\frac{1}{x}$ and $\frac{1}{x^2}$
        approach an axis but never reach it, so $x \ne 0$.
  \item Even-degree powers ($x^2$, $x^4$) have both arms up; odd ($x^3$) has the left arm down.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 2.B} --- Construct equivalent representations: absolute value as a
piecewise function. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item $|x| = -x$ for $x < 0$ and $x$ for $x \ge 0$; the break sits where the expression
        inside changes sign, so $|x - 2|$ breaks at $x = 2$.
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} ``$|x| = -x$ for $x < 0$ can't be right, because $-x$
        is negative.'' Students read $-x$ as \textit{a negative number}; it means \textit{the
        opposite of} $x$, and every input that rule owns is negative, so its output is
        positive. The two readings disagree exactly on the inputs the rule owns.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.24\linewidth} X @{}}
Imaginary unit     & The number $i = \sqrt{-1}$, so $i^2 = -1$. For $n > 0$, $\sqrt{-n} = i\sqrt{n}$. \\
Complex number     & A number $a + bi$ with $a$, $b$ real: $a$ is the \textbf{real part}, $b$ the \textbf{imaginary part}. \\
Complex conjugate  & The partner $a - bi$ of $a + bi$; their product $a^2 + b^2$ is always real. \\
Parent function    & The simplest function of a family, before any shift, stretch, or flip. \\
Key points         & Points with small whole-number coordinates that pin down a parent graph's shape. \\
Absolute value     & The distance from $x$ to 0, never negative: $|x| = -x$ for $x < 0$ and $x$ for $x \ge 0$. \\
Even / odd degree  & The exponent of a power function: even powers have both arms up, odd powers one down and one up. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review (5 min)]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: solving $x^2 = 49$ with both roots and simplifying a square root such as
    $\sqrt{50}$ (today's $\sqrt{-n} = i\sqrt{n}$ sits on top of it); multiplying two binomials
    (complex multiplication is the same FOIL); evaluating a piecewise function from 1.1,
    naming the rule that owns each input; and evaluating absolute values as numbers, including
    $-|4|$, before today writes $|x|$ as a piecewise function.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook (2 min)]{lilac}
    \textbf{The calculator says ERROR.} Have students type $\sqrt{-9}$ into a calculator.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Ask: is that because \textit{no number at all} squares to $-9$, or only no number
              \textit{they have met yet}? Take two answers; do not resolve it.
        \item Follow up: $3^2 = 9$ and $(-3)^2 = 9$ --- every real square is 0 or positive. So
              a number whose square is negative would have to be a new kind of number.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} mathematicians met exactly this wall in the 1500s solving
    equations, and their answer was to \textit{name} the missing number. Today it gets its
    name, and the rest of the course uses it.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- I Do / We Do / You Do]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{I DO --- Part 1: The Imaginary Unit and $a + bi$} (10 min)

\textit{Notes rows IMAGINARY UNIT and COMPLEX NUMBERS: read each definition, mark up the
display, work problems 1, 5, and 7. Teacher works; students watch and annotate. Nothing is
cold-called in this phase.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the IMAGINARY UNIT sentences aloud and write $i^2 = -1$ large on the board ---
        every computation today ends by using it.
  \item Mark up the cycle: fill $i^3 = -i$ (that is $i^2 \cdot i$) and $i^4 = 1$ (that is
        $i^2 \cdot i^2$) yourself, narrating the multiplication. Read the gold box, including
        the trap, and leave it for problem 4.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 1}: $\sqrt{-25} = 5i$, then $\sqrt{-12} = i\sqrt{12} = 2i\sqrt{3}$
        --- pull out the $i$ \textit{before} touching the 12.
  \item Read the COMPLEX NUMBERS sentences; fill the two label boxes on $7 - 2i$ (7 and $-2$
        --- the imaginary part is $-2$, not $-2i$). Work \textbf{problem 5} (like parts) and
        \textbf{problem 7} by the three steps, stopping visibly at $-3i^2$ to say ``$i^2$ is
        $-1$, so this is $+3$.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 2: Complex Practice} (6 min)

\textit{Problems 2--4, 6, 8. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 2}: two equations, two answers each --- insist on the $\pm$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 3}: divide by 4, use the remainder. \textbf{Problem 4} is the trap:
        let the class say why 6 is wrong before anyone computes $-6$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 6}: the minus sign reaches both parts. \textbf{Problem 8}: the
        conjugate product is 25 --- ask what happened to the $i$.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 3: The Library of Parent Functions} (9 min)

\textit{Notes row PARENT FUNCTIONS. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.
Teacher annotates panels A--C and works problem 9; the class annotates D--J and works 10.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the definition, then model the domain/range reading on panels A--C: domain left to
        right, range bottom to top. Panel A's range is \textit{only} $y = 2$.
  \item Cold-call panels D--J, one student per panel, both lines. Slow down on G ($x \ge 0$,
        $y \ge 0$) and on I and J: the graph approaches the axis but never reaches it, so
        $x \ne 0$ --- that sentence is all the asymptote theory today.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 9} from the key points alone ($(8, 2)$ is the giveaway: $2^3 = 8$);
        \textbf{problem 10} names even vs.\ odd from panels C, D, E.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 4: Absolute Value as a Piecewise Function} (8 min)

\textit{Notes row ABSOLUTE VALUE --- the spine of the lesson. Class fills the blanks together;
cold-call every one. Teacher works problem 11; the class works 12--14.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the definition and the cases aloud. Say the target sentence twice: \textit{$-x$
        means the opposite of $x$.} Point at the red half of the graph: every input there is
        negative, so every output is its opposite --- positive.
  \item Fill the $|x - 2|$ boxes yourself: ``where is $x - 2$ negative? When $x < 2$. That is
        where we take the opposite.'' Then work \textbf{problem 11}.
  \item \textbf{Problem 12} is the misconception as a table: $-x$ is 5, 1, 0, $-2$ --- positive
        exactly when $x < 0$. \textbf{Problem 13 is the crux}: test the classmate's claim at
        $x = -6$ and ask for the \textit{reason}. \textbf{Problem 14} shows the two readings
        disagreeing at $x = -1$: 3 versus $-3$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{YOU DO --- Part 5: All Three} (15 min)

\textit{Notes row ALL THREE, problems 15--18 --- the whole independent phase. Students work
alone; circulate and say nothing a neighbor could say instead.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 15} multiplies complex numbers; \textbf{16} solves $x^2 + 36 = 0$ and
        repeats the trap with new numbers.
  \item \textbf{Problem 17} identifies $\frac{1}{x^2}$ from a bare graph --- name, equation,
        domain, range.
  \item \textbf{Problem 18} is the crux transferred: $|x + 3|$ breaks at a \textit{negative}
        number, $x = -3$, and the rule $-(x + 3)$ must still give a positive output at
        $x = -5$.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Multiplying Complex Numbers]{lilac}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Rewrite any $\sqrt{-n}$ as $i\sqrt{n}$ first.
  \item Multiply like binomials (FOIL).
  \item Replace every $i^2$ with $-1$.
  \item Combine real parts and imaginary parts; write $a + bi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\textbf{Worked example} (notes problem 7):
\begin{align*}
(2 + 3i)(4 - i) &= 8 - 2i + 12i - 3i^2 \\
                &= 8 + 10i - 3(-1) \\
                &= 11 + 10i
\end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the \textbf{You Do} phase --- the fifteen minutes where students are working
problems 15--18 without you --- and check. The crux is \textbf{problem 13} (testing ``$-x$ is
negative'' at $x = -6$); its You Do transfer is \textbf{problem 18} ($|x + 3|$ at $x = -5$).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error:} leaving $i^2$ in the answer or replacing it with $+1$, so
        problem 15 comes out $5 - 2i$. Cold-call: ``What is $i^2$? Say it.''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} $\sqrt{-9}\cdot\sqrt{-16} = \sqrt{144} = 12$ in problem 16.
        Point at the gold trap box in row 1: ``Rewrite with $i$ first.''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} only $x = 6i$ in problem 16. Ask: ``What else squares to $-36$?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} calling the problem 17 graph $\frac{1}{x}$, or giving its range
        as all reals. Ask: ``Is any part of this graph below the $x$-axis?''
  \item \textbf{Common error (the crux):} in problem 18, putting $x + 3$ on the left of the
        break and $-(x + 3)$ on the right, because ``the minus sign should give the
        negatives.'' Push: ``Pick $x = -5$. What is $x + 3$ there? What is its opposite? Which
        one is a distance?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Differentiation --- During You Do (15 min)]{redbox}
There is no separate activity sheet: students work the notes' You Do row (problems 15--18)
alone while you circulate. Differentiate by \textit{where you stand}, not by handing out
different paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Support.} Sit with a struggling student on \textbf{problem 15} and have them
        write the four FOIL products as four separate terms before combining anything. The
        moment they write $-3i^2$, ask what $i^2$ is; most of the day's complex errors are
        that one step.
  \item \textbf{On level.} \textbf{Problems 16 and 17} should go unaided: 16 is problem 2 and
        problem 4 with new numbers, and 17 is a lookup in the gallery they just annotated. If a
        student asks which graph it is, point at the gallery and walk away.
  \item \textbf{Extend.} \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question --- the break is at a
        negative number and the rule $-(x + 3)$ must still give positive outputs. A student
        who finishes early: ask them to write $|2x - 6|$ piecewise (break at $x = 3$) and to
        say why the break is not at $x = 6$. Either answer presents in the Debrief.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (5 min)]{redbox}
Whole class, teacher-led, packets still open. Three moves, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Share out the You Do} (2 min) --- one answer per problem, not a full review.
        Problem 15: $11 - 2i$, with the $-3i^2 = +3$ step said aloud. Problem 16: $\pm 6i$ and
        $-12$. Problem 17: reciprocal squared, $x \ne 0$, $y > 0$. Problem 18: the two rules
        and $|-5 + 3| = 2$ --- if a student wrote 18 cleanly, they present it, because it is
        exactly the shape of the diagnostic DeltaMath topic.
  \item \textbf{Name the headline} (1 min) --- say it, then make the class say it back:
        \textit{Rewrite with $i$ before you compute. And $-x$ means the opposite of $x$ --- on
        the inputs it owns, it is always positive.} Point at the red half of the $|x|$ graph
        while you say the second sentence.
  \item \textbf{Point forward} (2 min) --- the DeltaMath assignment \textit{Lesson 2.1} and its
        due date (on the board; students copy it into cover row 4), then 2.2: it takes every
        card in the library and moves, stretches, and flips it; $|x - 2|$ is already one of
        those moves.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Do not} re-teach the powers of $i$ or the gallery here, or take new questions. With no
exit ticket, the You Do was the period's formative read. If it showed
absolute value did not land, that is the 2.2 warm-up's job, not this minute's.
\end{skillbox}

Section I is five AP-style multiple-choice items whose distractors are the lesson's errors:

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1(a) is the habit today's equations need: a
student who writes only $x = 7$ for $x^2 = 49$ will write only $3i$ for $x^2 = -9$. Item 2 is
the FOIL that complex multiplication runs on --- a student who cannot do it will not survive
problem 7, so note names. Item 3 is a 1.1 spiral: naming the rule that owns an input is the
exact move the ABSOLUTE VALUE row asks for. Item 4's $-|4| = -4$ is there on purpose: the
negative sign \textit{outside} the bars stays, which sets up the contrast with $-x$
\textit{inside} the rule, where a negative input makes it positive.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The notes are one Main Ideas / Notes table, four instruction rows and a You Do row. In each
instruction row you read the printed definition, mark up the display, and work the first
problem (1, 5 and 7, 9, 11); the class works the rest, every problem cold-called. On a day
this dense the danger is the complex rows eating the clock: they are procedural and students
like them, so hold Part 2 to six minutes and move. The IMAGINARY UNIT row lives on one habit
--- \textit{rewrite with $i$ first} --- and problem 4 is the trap that proves why.

The gallery is a reference students will reuse all of 2.2, so the printed names, equations,
and key points stay; only the domain and range lines are theirs to fill. Do not drift into
asymptotes beyond ``approaches but never reaches the axis.''

ABSOLUTE VALUE is the spine. The misconception is a reading error, not an arithmetic one:
students see the minus sign in $-x$ and hear ``negative.'' Problem 12 makes them compute $-x$
for four inputs before anyone argues; problem 13 asks for the \emph{reason}, not the number;
problem 14 puts the two readings side by side at $x = -1$ (3 versus $-3$) so the wrong one is
visibly impossible --- a distance cannot be negative. Problem 18 in the You Do is the same
idea with the break at a negative number, which is where it gets hard.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Debrief]
Five minutes, and on this lesson they are the most at risk in the unit --- the guided phase
runs 23 minutes and will try to borrow them. Borrow from problem 10 instead (state the even/odd
rule aloud; the DeltaMath parent-function topic meets it again) or from problem 3. The
share-out is one answer per You Do problem so a student who stalled on 15 still hears the
problem 18 result --- the exact shape of the diagnostic DeltaMath topic, which they will
otherwise meet alone at home. The headline has two sentences because the day has two halves;
if time forces one, keep the second. Never drop the DeltaMath assignment and due date: it is
the only place the class hears them.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
Extra credit, optional, and not a substitute for the DeltaMath assignment --- say so when you
hand out the packet. Score it however extra credit works in your gradebook; the cover gives it a $+$ cell
outside the total. Multiple-choice answers are C, B, D, A, B. Item 5 is the most useful one to
glance at: a student who circles (A) put $x - 5$ on the left of the break and $-(x - 5)$ on the
right --- the lesson's misconception exactly --- and a student who circles (C) distributed the
negative to only the $x$. Item 2's (A) 10 is the trap from notes problem 4. In the free
response, accept $40 - L$ or $-(L - 40)$ in (b); part (c) is the deepest question on the page
--- credit any answer that computes $-(37 - 40) = 3$ and says the minus sign takes the
opposite of a negative.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
DeltaMath, graded, assigned as \textit{Lesson 2.1}, due on the date you set. The diagnostic is
\textbf{Absolute Value as a Piecewise Function}: a miss there means the student put $x - 4$ and
$-(x - 4)$ on the wrong sides of the break, or got a negative value from an absolute value ---
the target misconception, met alone. Check that topic's scores before 2.2, which opens by moving
the $|x|$ corner. DeltaMath cannot test the \textit{reason} --- that $-x$ means the opposite of
$x$, so it is positive on the inputs it owns --- and if it substitutes Evaluate Piecewise
Functions it cannot test the misconception at all. \textbf{Problem 18} in the You Do carries it
($-(x + 3)$ at $x = -5$ gives 2); read it while you circulate, with problem 13 as the reasoning.
\textbf{Absolute Value as a Piecewise Function} and \textbf{Parent Functions: Identify from
Graph} predict 2.2; the complex topics do not return until complex zeros. If either slips,
open the 2.2 warm-up with $|x - 4|$ at $x = 1$ (answer 3, from $4 - x$) and one bare parent
graph to name; a class-wide miss on \textbf{Multiply Complex Numbers} ($i^2$ left as $+1$)
gets a one-minute check-in at the door instead.
\end{teachernote}
