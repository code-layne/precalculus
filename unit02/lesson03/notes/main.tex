% ── ROW 1: symmetry, read from the graph ─────────────────────────────────────
\mainidea[Two ways to match]{Symmetry} &
A function is \textbf{even} if its graph is a mirror image of itself over the $y$-axis: fold
along the $y$-axis and the two halves match. A function is \textbf{odd} if its graph is
symmetric about the origin: a half turn about $(0, 0)$ lands the graph exactly on itself. Most
functions are \textbf{neither}.
\\

% ── ROW 2: the algebraic test ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\mainidea[By algebra]{Even or Odd Test} &
To test with algebra, replace every $x$ with $(-x)$, simplify, and compare. If $f(-x) = f(x)$,
the function is even; if $f(-x) = -f(x)$, it is odd; if neither holds, it is neither. In 2.2,
$y = f(-x)$ was the reflection over the $y$-axis --- an even function is one that reflection
does not change.

\vspace{3pt}
\stepnum{1}\ Find $f(-x)$, with $-x$ in parentheses. \quad \stepnum{2}\ Simplify: an even
power erases the sign; an odd power keeps it. \quad \stepnum{3}\ Compare with $f(x)$ and
with $-f(x)$.
\\
& \begin{center}

% ── ROW 3: one-to-one, and the inverse read from tables and graphs ───────────
\mainidea[Tables and graphs]{Inverses} &
A function is \textbf{one-to-one} if each output comes from exactly one input; on a graph, no
horizontal line crosses it more than once --- the \textbf{horizontal line test}. A one-to-one
function has an \textbf{inverse} $f^{-1}$ that runs it backward: if $f(a) = b$, then
$f^{-1}(b) = a$. Each point $(a, b)$ of $f$ becomes the point $(b, a)$ of $f^{-1}$ --- a
reflection over the line $y = x$ --- so the domain of $f$ is the range of $f^{-1}$, and the range
of $f$ is the domain of $f^{-1}$.
\\

% ── ROW 4: THE CRUX — finding the inverse, and what f^{-1} is NOT ────────────
\mainidea[By algebra]{Finding the Inverse} &
To find $f^{-1}$ from a formula, undo the machine: \stepnum{1}\ write $y = f(x)$; \stepnum{2}\
swap $x$ and $y$; \stepnum{3}\ solve for $y$; \stepnum{4}\ rename $y$ as $f^{-1}(x)$. To check,
compose: $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$ and $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$ --- each undoes the other. A function that is
not one-to-one, like $x^2$, becomes invertible when we \textbf{restrict its domain}, keeping
only $x \ge 0$.
\\
& \begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small]
  \node[draw=plum, very thick, fill=lilac, rounded corners=3pt, text width=5.2cm,
        align=center] at (0,0) {\textbf{$f^{-1}(x)$ --- the INVERSE}\\[2pt]
        Runs $f$ backward. For $f(x) = x + 3$:\\[2pt] $f^{-1}(5) = 2$, because $f(2) = 5$.};
  \node[draw=redacc, very thick, fill=redbg, rounded corners=3pt, text width=5.2cm,
        align=center] at (6.0,0) {\textbf{$1/f(x)$ --- the RECIPROCAL}\\[2pt]
        One over the output. For $f(x) = x + 3$:\\[2pt] $\dfrac{1}{f(5)} = \dfrac{1}{8}$.};
  \node[font=\small\bfseries\color{redacc}] at (3.0,0) {$\ne$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\\
& \fcolorbox{plum}{lilac}{\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}{\centering
\textbf{The $-1$ in $f^{-1}$ names the inverse --- it is not an exponent. $f^{-1}$ undoes $f$:
it sends each output back to the input it came from.}}}
