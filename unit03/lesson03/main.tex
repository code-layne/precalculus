    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will treat a zero of a polynomial, a root of
    $p(x) = 0$, an $x$-intercept, and a linear factor $(x - a)$ as one fact said four ways;
    read each zero and its \textit{multiplicity} from a factored form; count every zero with
    the degree (a degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ complex zeros, counting multiplicity);
    name the conjugate partner of a non-real zero; and decide from the multiplicity whether the
    graph crosses the $x$-axis or touches it and turns back --- and so whether the polynomial
    changes sign there.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED 1.5.A --- EK 1.5.A.1--1.5.A.5)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{AP Skill 2.B} --- Construct equivalent representations: move among a zero, a root, an
$x$-intercept, and a linear factor. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item $p(a) = 0$, ``$a$ solves $p(x) = 0$,'' ``$(a, 0)$ is on the graph,'' and ``$(x - a)$
        is a factor'' are one fact for a real $a$ (EK 1.5.A.1, 1.5.A.3).
  \item The sign flips: the zero $-2$ belongs to the factor $(x + 2)$; and $2x - 3$ gives
        $\tfrac{3}{2}$, not 3.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 1.B} --- Express functions, equations, or expressions in analytically
equivalent forms: read the degree and each multiplicity from a factored form and count. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item The exponent on a factor is the zero's multiplicity; the exponents add to the degree.
  \item A degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ complex zeros counting multiplicity (EK
        1.5.A.2). A real number is complex too.
  \item With real coefficients, a non-real zero $a + bi$ brings its conjugate $a - bi$ (EK
        1.5.A.4). Non-real zeros make no $x$-intercept.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 2.B} --- Read multiplicity off a graph, and a graph off a multiplicity. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Odd multiplicity: the graph crosses and the sign changes (1 straight, 3 or more
        flattening). Even: it touches, turns back, is tangent to the axis, and the sign stays
        the same (EK 1.5.A.5).
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} \textit{every zero crosses the $x$-axis.} Students
        expect $(x - 2)^2(x + 1)$ to change sign at 2 the way $(x - 2)(x + 1)$ does.
        \textbf{Secondary:} ``degree 4 but only 2 intercepts, so 2 zeros are missing'' --- they
        are a repeated zero or a non-real conjugate pair.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Zero of a polynomial & A number $a$ with $p(a) = 0$. For a real $a$, $(a, 0)$ is an $x$-intercept and $(x - a)$ is a factor. \\
Root & A solution of the equation $p(x) = 0$ --- the same number as a zero of $p$. \\
Linear factor & A factor of the form $(x - a)$; it contributes the zero $a$. \\
Multiplicity & The number of times $(x - a)$ appears as a factor; the exponent on that factor. \\
Fundamental Theorem of Algebra & A polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ has exactly $n$ complex zeros, counting multiplicity. \\
Conjugate pair & $a + bi$ and $a - bi$. A polynomial with real coefficients has its non-real zeros in such pairs. \\
Tangent to the $x$-axis & Touching the axis at a zero and turning back without crossing --- the picture of an even multiplicity. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review (5 min)]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review). This is the first lesson after winter break,
    so the warm-up re-activates the two lessons today stands on. From 3.2: reading the degree
    and leading coefficient of a polynomial written in factored form by multiplying only the
    leading terms, stating its end behavior in limit language, and recalling that a degree-$n$
    polynomial has \textit{at most} $n$ real zeros and $n - 1$ turning points. From 2.1:
    writing the square root of a negative number with $i$ ($\sqrt{-9}$, $\sqrt{-20}$), and
    adding and multiplying the conjugate pair $2 \pm 3i$ --- both results come out real, which
    is the reason conjugate pairs show up today.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook (3 min)]{lilac}
    \textbf{Two zeros went missing.} Put the hook graph on the screen: a degree-4 polynomial
    $H$ whose graph meets the $x$-axis only at $x = -1$ and $x = 3$.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Ask what 3.2 promised about a degree-4 polynomial. Take the answer ``at most 4
              real zeros'' and write \textit{at most} large on the board.
        \item Then: ``This one shows two. Did it lose two? Where could they be?'' Collect two
              or three guesses --- ``they're off the screen'' is the common one, and the end
              behavior on the graph rules it out. Do not resolve it.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} leave the question on the board. Problem 14 answers it ---
    $H$ still has exactly four zeros; two of them are a non-real conjugate pair the graph cannot
    show --- and the Debrief points back to it.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- I Do / We Do / You Do]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{I DO --- Part 1: Zeros and Multiplicity} (10 min)

\textit{Notes rows ZEROS and MULTIPLICITY: read each definition, mark up the display, work
problems 1 and 5. Teacher works; students watch and annotate. Nothing is cold-called in this
phase.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the four statements aloud and point at the graph of
        $p(x) = (x + 2)(x - 1)(x - 4)$ for each: $p(1) = 0$, 1 solves $p(x) = 0$, the dot at
        $(1, 0)$, the factor $(x - 1)$. Fill the table's $-2$ row yourself, saying the sign
        flip out loud: zero $-2$, factor $(x + 2)$.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 1}: $p(4) = 0$ is a zero; $p(0) = 8$ is the $y$-intercept, not a
        zero --- the two are confused all year.
  \item Read the MULTIPLICITY sentences. Fill the ledger for $f(x) = 2(x - 3)^2(x + 1)^3$:
        exponents are multiplicities, $2 + 3 = 5$ is the degree, the 2 out front makes no zero.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 5}, $g(x) = (x + 4)(x - 2)^3$, ending on the count: four zeros,
        degree 4.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 2: Reading and Counting} (6 min)

\textit{Problems 2--4 and 6. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 2}: $p(2) = -8$, so 2 is not a zero and $(x - 2)$ is not a factor.
  \item \textbf{Problem 3} is the trap $2x - 3$: the zero is $\tfrac32$, and $x$ alone gives 0.
  \item \textbf{Problem 4} runs the four statements backwards from $f(-6) = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 6}: the $x^2$ gives a zero of multiplicity 2 at 0; the $-3$ gives none.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 3: Conjugate Pairs} (7 min)

\textit{Notes row CONJUGATE PAIRS. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.
Teacher works problem 7; the class works 8--10.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Connect to warm-up item 4: $2 + 3i$ and $2 - 3i$ multiply to a real number. That is
        why they travel together as zeros of a real polynomial.
  \item The display: $x^2 + 4$ never meets the axis, yet solving gives $\pm 2i$ --- fill the
        two label boxes. Two zeros, no intercepts.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 7} ($2 - 3i$). \textbf{Problem 8} solves $x^2 + 9 = 0$;
        \textbf{9} counts $3, 3, 1 \pm i$ to the degree. \textbf{Problem 10} (a cubic has a
        real zero) is the first thing to cut.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 4: Cross or Touch} (8 min)

\textit{Notes row CROSS OR TOUCH --- the crux. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call
every one. Teacher works problem 11; the class works 12--14.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Put the three graphs side by side and say what differs: \textit{only} the exponent on
        $(x - 2)$. Fill the three label boxes: crosses; touches and turns back; flattens and
        crosses.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 11}: test $1.9$ and $2.1$ in $P_1$ --- negative, then positive.
        \textbf{Problem 12 is the crux}: the same test in $P_2$ gives positive twice. Ask
        \textit{why} before anyone writes: a square is never negative.
  \item \textbf{Problem 13} matches the description to a factored form; \textbf{14} answers the
        hook --- point at the board.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{YOU DO --- Part 5: Read the Zeros} (16 min)

\textit{Notes row READ THE ZEROS, problems 15--18 --- the whole independent phase. Students
work alone; circulate and say nothing a neighbor could say instead.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 15} reads $f(x) = x(x + 4)^2(x - 3)^3$ completely; \textbf{16} is the
        crux transferred --- the sign on both sides of $-4$, with no graph to look at.
  \item \textbf{Problem 17} matches the graph of $w$ to one of four factored forms, each wrong
        choice one of the day's errors.
  \item \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question: a degree-5 graph with one straight
        crossing and one given non-real zero --- the secondary misconception, counted to the
        end.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the \textbf{You Do} phase --- the sixteen minutes where students are working
problems 15--18 without you --- and check. The crux is \textbf{problem 12} ($P_2$ is positive
on both sides of 2); its You Do transfer is \textbf{problem 16} ($f$ is positive on both sides
of $-4$, from the factored form alone).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error (the crux):} ``every zero crosses,'' so $f$ must change sign at
        $-4$. Cold-call: ``What is the exponent on $(x + 4)$? Can $(x + 4)^2$ ever be
        negative?'' Point at the $P_2$ graph they labelled.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} the sign flip --- reading the zero of $(x + 4)$ as 4, or the
        zero of $(x - 3)^3$ as $-3$. Ask: ``What makes that factor equal 0?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} counting distinct zeros and calling it the degree (3 zeros, so
        degree 3, in problem 15). Ask for the exponents, then their sum.
  \item \textbf{Common error (secondary):} on problem 18, stopping at ``2, $1 - 2i$, $1 + 2i$''
        with two zeros unaccounted for. Ask: ``Degree 5 --- how many have you listed? Can the
        missing ones be real?''
  \item \textbf{Problem 17:} choosing (A) because it ``has a square in it.'' Ask which factor the
        square is on, and where that zero is on the graph.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Differentiation --- During You Do (16 min)]{redbox}
There is no separate activity sheet: students work the notes' You Do row (problems 15--18)
alone while you circulate. Differentiate by \textit{where you stand}, not by handing out
different paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Support.} Sit with a struggling student on \textbf{problem 15} and make a ledger
        like row 2's: factor, zero, multiplicity --- one line per factor --- before any word
        about crossing. Then send them to the row 4 label boxes to translate each
        multiplicity into a picture.
  \item \textbf{On level.} \textbf{Problem 16} is the crux check. Do not answer it; ask for the
        sign of each factor at $x = -4.1$, then walk away. The class should clear 15--17
        unaided.
  \item \textbf{Extend.} \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question: one real zero of
        multiplicity 1 in a degree-5 polynomial forces four non-real zeros --- two pairs. A
        student who says the second pair \emph{could} repeat $1 \pm 2i$ has the count exactly
        right. Hand a finisher who is done: ``Could a degree-5 graph with real coefficients
        cross nowhere?'' (No --- odd degree leaves one real zero.)
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard[20]
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (5 min)]{redbox}
Whole class, teacher-led, packets still open. Three moves, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Share out the You Do} (3 min) --- one answer per problem, not a full review.
        Problem 15: degree 6, and the three behaviors. Problem 16: positive on both sides of
        $-4$, with the reason --- a square is never negative. Problem 17: (C). Problem 18: four
        non-real zeros. If a student explains 18's count cleanly, they present it; it is the
        hook's question answered at degree 5.
  \item \textbf{Name the headline} (1 min) --- say it, then make the class say it back:
        \textit{every factor is a zero, the exponents count them to the degree, and only an odd
        exponent crosses --- an even one touches and turns back.} Point at the $P_2$ graph
        while you say it, then at the hook graph: its other two zeros were a conjugate pair.
  \item \textbf{Point forward} (1 min) --- the DeltaMath assignment, \textit{Lesson 3.3}, and
        its due date: write it on the board and have students copy it into row 4 of the
        cover. Then 3.4: today every polynomial arrived already factored; next time we do the
        factoring ourselves.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Do not} re-teach conjugate pairs here or take new questions. If the share-out shows
the crux did not land, a sign-test item like problem 16 opens the 3.4 warm-up.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators --- and it is the first thing students do after
three weeks away, so expect it to be slow and do not rescue it early. Items 1--2 are 3.2: a
student who multiplies out the whole of $-2x(x - 1)(x + 3)$ to find the degree has forgotten
that only the leading terms matter, and will need the same shortcut for every degree today.
Item 2's ``at most 5'' is the phrase the hook turns on --- listen for it. Items 3--4 are 2.1:
$\sqrt{-20} = 2i\sqrt{5}$ catches anyone who writes $\sqrt{-20} = -2\sqrt{5}$. Item 4(c) is
the seed of row 3 --- the sum and product of a conjugate pair are both real --- so read one
answer aloud as you start the hook.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The notes are one Main Ideas / Notes table, four instruction rows and a You Do row; in each
instruction row you read the printed sentences, mark up the display, and work the first
problem of the grid (1, 5, 7, 11), and the class works the rest with the pen in their hand,
every problem cold-called. ZEROS lives on the sign flip: say ``the zero $-2$ goes with
$(x + 2)$'' every time it comes up, and use problem 1 to separate a zero from the
$y$-intercept. MULTIPLICITY is bookkeeping --- the ledger is the habit, and the count must
land on the degree every time. CONJUGATE PAIRS should feel short because the warm-up did the
arithmetic; resist solving anything harder than $x^2 + 9 = 0$ (3.4 owns the rest).

CROSS OR TOUCH is the spine. The three graphs differ only in the exponent on $(x - 2)$; make
the class say so before any labelling. Problem 12 asks for the \emph{reason} the sign does not
change, not just the two signs: $(x - 2)^2$ is never negative. Problem 14 closes the hook ---
do it with the hook graph on the screen.

The You Do row (15--18) is the entire independent phase and carries sixteen minutes. Problem
16 is the crux with the graph taken away; problem 18 is the secondary misconception at full
strength. If the clock slips, cut problem 10 first and problem 13 next; never cut 12.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Debrief]
Five minutes, and on the first day back they are at risk --- the warm-up runs slow and the
guided phase will try to borrow from the end. Borrow from problem 10 or 13 instead, never from
the You Do or these minutes: the share-out is the only place a student who stalled on 15 hears
that $f$ stays positive across $-4$ --- the exact question the diagnostic DeltaMath topic will
ask them alone at home. The headline must point back at the hook graph; students who leave
believing two zeros were ``missing'' will carry that into 3.4's factoring, where a quadratic
factor like $x^2 + 4$ is the whole point. The point-forward minute is the only time the
DeltaMath due date is said aloud.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
Extra credit, optional, scored 1 to 5, and not a substitute for the DeltaMath assignment ---
say so when you hand out the packet. Its 1-to-5 score is added as extra-credit points.
Multiple-choice answers are \textbf{D, B, C, A, C}. Item 1: (A) flips the sign of the factor,
(B) and (C) read $R(4) = 0$ as a $y$-intercept or a height of 4. Item 2: (A) counts factors
instead of adding exponents, (C) counts repeated zeros as different, (D) misses the factor $x$.
Item 3 is the most useful one to glance at: (D) is ``every zero crosses'' --- the lesson's
misconception --- and (B) puts the touch on the wrong zero. Item 4: (B) and (D) negate the real
part. Item 5: (A) means the student thinks the degree is only a maximum, (B) that non-real
zeros need not pair, (D) that the two real zeros do not count. In the free response, accept
the zeros in any order in (a); part (b) is the crux in context --- credit any answer that says
the sign of $h$ does not change at the even-multiplicity zero 2, so the dolphin is under water
on both sides. Part (c) needs both the conjugate $4 - i$ and the count of real zeros (two
surface times); either alone earns 1.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
DeltaMath, graded, assigned as \textit{Lesson 3.3} and due on the date you set.
\textbf{Graphical Effect Multiplicity L1} is the diagnostic: check its item-level results
before 3.4. A miss on a touching zero means the student still believes every zero crosses ---
problem 12 did not land; a miss on a crossing zero is usually the sign flip, so look at
\textbf{Find Multiplicity of Zeroes (Polynomials)} for the same student first. DeltaMath cannot
ask \emph{why} the sign stays the same at an even zero: that reason lives in You Do problem 16,
so read it on the packet when you collect it. \textbf{Solve Factored Equation} and
\textbf{Find Multiplicity of Zeroes (Polynomials)} predict 3.4, where students factor first and
then read the zeros exactly as today; \textbf{Complex Conjugate Theorem} predicts the
$x^2 + k$ factors 3.4 and 3.6 lean on. If any of them slips, reuse a one-line item like
problem 3 or 16 in the 3.4 warm-up rather than re-teaching.
\end{teachernote}
