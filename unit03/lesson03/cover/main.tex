\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item I can explain why a zero of a polynomial, a root of $p(x) = 0$, an $x$-intercept of its
        graph, and a linear factor $(x - a)$ all describe the same number, and read the zeros
        from a factored form.
  \item I can find the multiplicity of each zero and use the degree to count every zero:
        a polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ complex zeros, counting multiplicity.
  \item I can name the conjugate of a non-real zero of a polynomial with real coefficients.
  \item I can tell from the multiplicity whether a graph crosses the $x$-axis or touches it and
        turns back --- and whether the polynomial changes sign there.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small
A \textbf{zero} of a polynomial is an input that makes the output 0. When the polynomial is
written in \textbf{factored form}, every linear factor hands you a zero --- and a factor that
repeats gives that zero a \textbf{multiplicity}. Count every zero with its multiplicity and you
get exactly the \textbf{degree}. Some zeros are not real; with real coefficients they arrive in
\textbf{conjugate pairs} and never show up as $x$-intercepts. And not every zero crosses the
axis: at a zero of even multiplicity the graph touches and turns back.
\end{remindbox}
