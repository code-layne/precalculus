\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{AP Skill 2.A} --- Identify information from graphical, numerical, analytical, and
verbal representations to answer a question. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item In a table whose inputs go up by \textbf{equal steps}, the least $n$ for which the
        $n$th successive differences are constant is the degree of the polynomial that fits.
        Unequal steps make the differences meaningless. (1.5.A.6)
  \item A polynomial is written in standard form; its $y$-intercept is $(0, p(0))$, and $p(0)$
        is the constant term. (1.4.A.1)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function with varying levels of
precision. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item The average rate of change on $[a, b]$ is the slope of the secant line; for a polynomial
        of degree 2 or more it changes from interval to interval. The difference quotient is
        that rate on $[x, x + h]$. (1.3.A)
  \item Where the rate of change is increasing the graph is concave up; where it is decreasing,
        concave down; an inflection point is where it switches. (1.3.B.3, 1.4.A.5)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 3.B} --- Describe the assumptions and limitations of a model (here: a
polynomial used only on a restricted domain, where the endpoints compete for the absolute
extrema --- notes problems 14 and 18). &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A relative extremum is a turn: the highest or lowest output \emph{nearby}. Between two
        distinct real zeros there is always one. (1.4.A.2, 1.4.A.3)
  \item An even-degree polynomial has an absolute maximum or an absolute minimum; an odd-degree
        one, on all real numbers, has neither. On a restricted domain the endpoints compete
        for absolute. (1.4.A.2, 1.4.A.4)
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} \emph{``a relative maximum is the highest point of the
        graph.''} Notes problem 12 sets the cubic's relative maximum 6 against $p(5) = 22$, and
        problem 13 the quartic's two valleys, only the lower of which is the absolute minimum.
        Its You Do transfer is problem 18.
  \item \textbf{Second trap:} successive differences taken on a table whose input steps are not
        equal --- notes problem 4.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Successive differences & In a table with equally spaced inputs, the differences of consecutive outputs (first differences), then of those (second differences), and so on. \\
Degree from a table & The least $n$ for which the $n$th differences are constant: constant first differences, linear; second, quadratic; third, cubic. \\
Average rate of change & On $[a, b]$: \TallMath{\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}}, the slope of the secant line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. \\
Difference quotient & \TallMath{\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}}, $h \ne 0$: the average rate of change on $[x, x + h]$. \\
$y$-intercept of a polynomial & $(0, p(0))$; $p(0)$ is the constant term. \\
Increasing / decreasing & As the inputs increase, the outputs increase / decrease; written as open intervals of $x$-values. \\
Concave up / concave down & The rate of change is increasing / decreasing; the graph bends like a cup / a cap. \\
Point of inflection & A point where the rate of change switches between increasing and decreasing --- where the concavity changes. \\
Relative (local) maximum / minimum & An output greater / less than every nearby output; where the graph switches direction. \\
Absolute (global) maximum / minimum & The greatest / least output on the whole domain. An odd-degree polynomial on all real numbers has neither. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook (2 min)]{lilac}
    \textbf{Is the first hill the top?} A roller coaster's first hill is 40 meters tall.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item On the board: Jay says, ``So 40 meters is the highest the coaster ever goes.'' Ask:
              is Jay right? What would you need to see to be sure?
        \item Push until someone says it: the first hill is only the highest point \emph{near
              it}. A later hill could be taller --- you would need the heights over the whole
              ride.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} in 2.5 we called the first hill a \emph{relative} maximum. Today
    we find out when a polynomial's relative maximum is also its highest point overall --- and
    when the graph has no highest point at all.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- I Do / We Do / You Do]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{I DO --- Part 1: Differences and Rates} (10 min)

\textit{Notes rows SUCCESSIVE DIFFERENCES and RATES OF CHANGE: read each definition, mark up the
difference table and the secant, work problems 1 and 5. Teacher works; students watch and
annotate. Nothing is cold-called in this phase.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the first row aloud. On the table of $p$, draw the little V's between neighbors and
        say each subtraction: ``6 minus 2 is 4, 4 minus 6 is $-2$\ldots'' --- then the second row,
        then the third. Fill the label boxes: \textit{third}, \textit{3}.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 1} the same way, out loud. Before subtracting, \emph{point at the
        $x$-row and say} ``steps of 1 --- equal, so differences are allowed.'' That sentence is
        problem 4's whole lesson.
  \item Read the RATES OF CHANGE row; on the graph, the run is 1 and the rise is 4, so the
        secant slope is 4 --- the first difference in the table. Work \textbf{problem 5}:
        $\frac{4 - 2}{2} = 1$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 2: Differences and Rates, Together} (8 min)

\textit{Problems 2--4 and 6--8. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 2} is linear at the first row; \textbf{problem 3} needs three rows.
        \textbf{Problem 4 is the trap}: Kim's first differences 2, 4, 2, 4 come from steps of 1,
        2, 1, 2. Divide each by its step and every rate is 2 --- $m$ \emph{is} linear.
  \item \textbf{Problem 6}: $-2$, decreasing on average. \textbf{Problem 7} is 2.6's difference
        quotient on a quadratic; insist on $f(x + h)$ written in full before anything is
        subtracted. \textbf{Problem 8} checks it against an ordinary average rate.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 3: The Shape of $p$} (6 min)

\textit{Notes row SHAPE OF $p$. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one. Teacher
works problem 9; the class works the two label boxes and problem 10.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Work \textbf{problem 9}: $p(0) = 2$, the constant term, is the red point. Then the
        intervals, narrating the axis: ``where it rises, in $x$'' --- $(-\infty, 1)$ and
        $(3, \infty)$; decreasing on $(1, 3)$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 10} ties concavity to rates (EK 1.3.B.3): the first differences fall
        from 4 to $-2$ and then rise to 4 and 16, and the switch sits at $x = 2$, the gold point.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 4: Relative vs.\ Absolute} (9 min)

\textit{Notes row RELATIVE VS.\ ABSOLUTE --- the crux. Class fills the blanks together;
cold-call every one. Teacher works problem 11; the class works 12--14.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Work \textbf{problem 11} as sentences: ``a relative maximum of 6 at $x = 1$; a
        relative minimum of 2 at $x = 3$.''
  \item \textbf{Problem 12 is the crux}: compute $p(5) = 22$ on the board, next to the 6. Ask
        where the right arrow goes. The cubic has no absolute maximum at all.
  \item \textbf{Problem 13} is the even-degree twin: two valleys, $-2$ and $-5$; only the
        lower one is the absolute minimum, and with both ends rising there is no absolute
        maximum. \textbf{Problem 14} restricts $p$ to $[0, 5]$: the endpoint wins, 22 at $x = 5$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{YOU DO --- Part 5: A New Stock} (15 min)

\textit{Notes row A NEW STOCK, problems 15--18 --- the whole independent phase. Students work
alone; circulate and say nothing a neighbor could say instead.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 15}: successive differences, degree 3. \textbf{Problem 16}: two rates,
        with units --- $+10$ and $-2$ dollars per month.
  \item \textbf{Problem 17} reads direction, concavity, and the inflection point $(3, 23)$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 18} is the crux transferred, and the finisher's question: the \$25 peak
        is only relative; the six-month high is \$41 at the endpoint $t = 6$.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Successive Differences]{lilac}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Check the inputs: equal steps? If not, stop --- differences do not apply.
  \item Subtract each output from the \emph{next} one (right minus left): first differences.
  \item Repeat on the new row: second differences; again, third.
  \item The first row that is all one number names the degree.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\textbf{Example} (notes problem 1): $f(x)$ = 1, 3, 9, 19, 33 at $x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$.
Steps of 1 --- equal. First differences 2, 6, 10, 14; second 4, 4, 4, constant. So a polynomial
of degree 2 fits $f$. (It is $f(x) = 2x^2 + 1$.)
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the \textbf{You Do} phase --- the fifteen minutes where students are working
problems 15--18 without you --- and check. The crux is \textbf{problems 12 and 13} (a relative
maximum is not the highest point; only the lower valley is the absolute minimum); its You Do
transfer is \textbf{problem 18}. The second trap is \textbf{problem 4} (unequal steps); its
transfer is the step check in \textbf{problem 15}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error (the target misconception):} ``the stock's maximum is \$25.''
        Point at the right end of the graph and ask: ``What was it worth in month 6?'' Then:
        ``Is \$25 the highest \emph{nearby}, or the highest of all?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} the absolute minimum given as the relative minimum, \$21 at
        $t = 4$, without checking the start. Point at the dot at $t = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} subtracting left minus right, so every difference has the wrong
        sign. The degree survives; the rates in problem 16 do not. Ask: ``Did the price go up
        from month 0 to month 2?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} intervals written in prices --- ``increasing on $(5, 25)$.'' Ask
        2.5's question: ``Is 25 a month or a price?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} a rate with no units, or ``\$20'' for $[0, 2]$ (the change, not
        divided by 2). Ask: ``Per what?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Differentiation --- During You Do (15 min)]{redbox}
There is no separate activity sheet: students work the notes' You Do row (problems 15--18)
alone while you circulate. Differentiate by \textit{where you stand}, not by handing out
different paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Support.} Sit with a struggling student on \textbf{problem 15} and have them
        write the differences \emph{under} the table, V-shaped as in row 1, saying each
        subtraction aloud. Then point at the graph for 17: trace it with a pencil, ``up, turn,
        down, turn, up,'' writing the month under each turn.
  \item \textbf{On level.} \textbf{Problems 16 and 17} should be done unaided: two rates with
        units, then three readings off the graph. Check only that each interval is in months.
  \item \textbf{Extend.} \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question. A student who finishes
        it gets: ``If the model kept going past month 6, would $S$ ever have an absolute
        maximum? What about a degree-4 model?'' --- the odd/even distinction from row 4, said
        in words. Either answer presents in the Debrief.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (5 min)]{redbox}
Whole class, teacher-led, packets still open. Three moves, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Share out the You Do} (3 min) --- one answer per problem, not a full review.
        Problem 15: ``third differences 6, 6 --- degree 3.'' Problem 16: ``\$10 per month, then
        $-\$2$ per month.'' Problem 17: the inflection point $(3, 23)$. Problem 18: ``\$25 is a
        relative maximum; the absolute maximum is \$41, at month 6.'' If a student answered the
        Extend question, they say it now --- no DeltaMath topic asks it in words.
  \item \textbf{Name the headline} (1 min) --- say it, then make the class say it back:
        \textit{a relative maximum is the highest point nearby, not the highest point of all ---
        check the ends of the graph. Equal steps first, then the differences tell the degree.}
        Point at the right arrow of the cubic while you say it.
  \item \textbf{Point forward} (1 min) --- the DeltaMath assignment \textit{Lesson 3.1} and its
        due date: write the date on the board and have students copy it into cover row 4.
        Then: in 3.2 we read the \emph{ends} of a polynomial straight from its degree and
        leading coefficient --- the arrows that decided today's absolute extrema.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Do not} re-teach successive differences here or take new questions. If the You Do showed
it did not land, it is the 3.2 warm-up's job, not this minute's.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1(b) is the one to watch: $(-1)^3$ and
$-2(-1)^2$ are both negative, and a student who gets $f(-1) = 6$ or $-6$ will stall on every
evaluation of $p$ today --- problem 12's $p(5)$ above all. Item 2 is 2.6's average rate on a
quadratic, which the RATES OF CHANGE row assumes cold. Item 3 reads intervals as $x$-values and
the maximum as a value at a location --- 2.5's distinction, which problem 11 uses as a sentence.
Item 4 adds $\infty$: polynomial intervals run off the edge of the graph, and a student who
writes $(-3, 1)$ for ``increasing'' in item 3 has read the grid's edge as the end of the graph.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The notes are one Main Ideas / Notes table, four instruction rows and a You Do row; in each
instruction row you read the printed definition, mark up the display, and work the first problem
of the grid (1, 5, 9, 11), and the class works the rest with the pen in their hand, every problem
cold-called. One polynomial, $p(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 2$, carries rows 1--4 and is drawn three
times on purpose: its table of differences, its secant, its shape, its turns --- so no student
flips pages to find it. Rows 2 and 3 are recall from 2.5 and 2.6; move briskly there and spend
the saved minutes on rows 1 and 4.

Successive differences are new. Insist on right-minus-left, and make students say ``equal steps''
out loud before every table: problem 4's whole point is that Kim's arithmetic is correct and her
conclusion is wrong, because the steps were 1, 2, 1, 2. RELATIVE VS.\ ABSOLUTE is the lesson.
Problem 12 must be computed, not argued: put $p(5) = 22$ beside the 6 and let the number end it.
Problem 13 is the even-degree twin --- two valleys, one absolute minimum --- and the place to say
that a degree-4 graph with both ends up \emph{must} have a lowest point. In this course a relative
extremum is a turn; endpoints compete only for absolute (problem 14), as in 2.5. If the clock
slips, cut problem 3 or problem 8, never 12 or 13.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Debrief]
Five minutes, and on this lesson they are at risk --- eight ideas share one guided phase and it
will try to borrow them. Borrow from problem 3 (a twin of problem 1) or problem 8 (a check)
instead; DeltaMath drills both again. Never borrow from the You Do: with no exit ticket,
circulating it is the last look you get before the work leaves the room. The share-out is one
answer per You Do problem, said as a sentence, so that a student who stalled on 16 still hears
``\$25 is a relative maximum; the absolute maximum is \$41, at month 6'' --- the shape the
DeltaMath extrema topic expects, which they will otherwise meet alone at home.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
Extra credit, optional, scored 1 to 5, and not a substitute for the DeltaMath assignment --- say
so when you hand out the packet. Its 1-to-5 score is added as extra-credit points.
Multiple-choice answers are \textbf{B, D, C, A, C}. Item 4 is the one to glance at: a student who
writes (B) took the later peak (or the one nearer the middle) as the absolute maximum, and one who
writes (C) called a relative minimum absolute on a graph whose ends fall forever --- the crux
exactly. Item 1 (C) means the student counted the row of zeros as a degree. In the free response,
accept $-1$ hundred people per hour or $-100$ people per hour in (b); part (c) earns full credit
only if it names $t = 0$ as the minimum \emph{and} says why the organizer was wrong.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
DeltaMath, graded, due on the date you set (assignment \textit{Lesson 3.1}). The diagnostic is
\textbf{Relative vs.\ Absolute Extrema}: a miss there is almost always the target misconception
--- the highest turn entered as the absolute maximum, or an absolute extremum claimed for a graph
whose ends run off forever. If it is common, open 3.2 with a warm-up item that shows a cubic and
asks, ``Does it have a highest point?'' --- 3.2's end behavior is the answer. A miss on
\textbf{Successive Differences (Polynomial)} that reports one degree too high is the row of zeros
counted; reuse notes problem 1 in that warm-up. DeltaMath cannot ask a student to explain
\emph{why} a relative maximum is not the highest point, so read \textbf{You Do problem 18} in the
packets as well. \textbf{Relative vs.\ Absolute Extrema} and \textbf{Points of Inflection from a
Graph} predict 3.2: a student who reads the ends of a graph to decide whether an absolute
extremum exists is already reasoning about end behavior.
\end{teachernote}
