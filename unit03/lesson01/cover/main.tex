\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item I can use \textbf{successive differences} in a table with equally spaced inputs to find
        the \textbf{degree} of the polynomial that fits it.
  \item I can find the \textbf{average rate of change} of a polynomial on an interval as the
        slope of a secant line, and simplify a \textbf{difference quotient}.
  \item I can tell from how the rate of change is changing whether a graph is \textbf{concave
        up} or \textbf{concave down}, and locate its \textbf{points of inflection}.
  \item I can read a polynomial's \textbf{$y$-intercept}, its intervals of \textbf{increasing}
        and \textbf{decreasing}, and its \textbf{relative} and \textbf{absolute extrema} --- and
        explain why a relative maximum need not be the highest point of the graph.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small
A table with equal steps tells you a polynomial's \textbf{degree}: keep subtracting until the
differences are constant. The \textbf{average rate of change} is the slope of a secant line,
and when the rate of change is \textbf{increasing} the graph is \textbf{concave up}. A
\textbf{relative} maximum is only the highest point \emph{nearby}: the \textbf{absolute}
maximum is the highest point of the whole graph --- and an odd-degree polynomial has none.
\end{remindbox}
