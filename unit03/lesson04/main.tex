    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will factor a polynomial completely by a strategy ---
    the greatest common factor first, then a difference of squares, a sum or difference of
    cubes, a trinomial or quadratic form, or grouping --- and solve a polynomial equation by
    moving every term to one side, factoring, and applying the Zero Product Property, finding
    every solution, real and non-real. They read the zeros, multiplicities, and $x$-intercepts
    from the factored form and check that a degree-$n$ equation has $n$ solutions --- never
    dividing both sides by $x$.
    \hfill\textit{\small (CED Topics 1.11 and 1.5 --- Skills 1.A, 1.B)}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{AP Skill 1.A} --- Solve equations and inequalities represented analytically, with
and without technology. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A product is zero exactly when one of its factors is zero, so a polynomial equation
        is solved by moving every term to one side, factoring, and setting each factor equal
        to zero. (1.11.A.1)
  \item $a$ is a zero of $p$ exactly when $(x - a)$ is a factor; each real zero is an
        $x$-intercept, and the non-real zeros come in conjugate pairs. (1.5.A.1)
  \item Counting multiplicity, a degree-$n$ polynomial equation has exactly $n$ solutions ---
        the check that none was lost. (1.5.A.2)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 1.B} --- Construct equivalent analytical representations. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item Factoring follows a strategy: GCF first; then count the terms --- two (squares, cubes),
        three (trinomial, quadratic form with $u = x^2$), four (grouping); then factor
        completely and check by multiplying.
  \item The factored form and the standard form are the same polynomial; each answers
        different questions --- the factored form shows the zeros, the standard form the
        degree and leading term. (1.11.A.3)
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{The distinction the lesson is built on.} &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} dividing both sides by $x$. On $x^3 = 9x$, students
        divide by $x$, get $x = \pm 3$, and lose $x = 0$; on $2x^4 = 8x^2$ dividing by $2x^2$
        loses the double zero at 0. Problems 11, 12 and 14 put the divide path and the factor
        path side by side so the two answers disagree; You Do problem 16 transfers it.
  \item \textbf{Second trap:} stopping early. $x^4 - 16 = (x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4)$ is not complete,
        and setting only $x^2 - 4 = 0$ gives two of the four solutions. Problem 13 fixes it;
        You Do problem 17 transfers it, and 18 needs both.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.27\linewidth} X @{}}
Zero Product Property & If $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. \\
Greatest common factor (GCF) & The largest factor --- a number and a power of $x$ --- that divides every term. \\
Factor by grouping & For four terms: factor each pair, then factor out the binomial the pairs share. \\
Difference of squares & $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$. A \emph{sum} of squares, $a^2 + b^2$, is prime over the reals. \\
Sum / difference of cubes & \TallMath{a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2), \quad a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)} \\
Quadratic form & A polynomial shaped like $au^2 + bu + c$ for $u = x^2$, such as $x^4 - 5x^2 + 4$. \\
Factored completely & Every factor is prime over the real numbers: none can be factored further. \\
Multiplicity & The number of times a factor $(x - a)$ repeats; a degree-$n$ equation has $n$ solutions counting it (3.3). \\
Conjugate pair & Non-real solutions $a + bi$ and $a - bi$; they arrive together (3.3). \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook (2 min)]{lilac}
    \textbf{You lost one.} Write Jordan's work on the board.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Jordan solves $x^3 = 4x$ by dividing both sides by $x$: $x^2 = 4$, so $x = \pm 2$.
              Riley says, ``You lost one.'' Ask: how many solutions should a degree-3
              equation have? (3.3: three, counting multiplicity.)
        \item Let the class hunt for 30 seconds. Push until someone tries $x = 0$:
              $0^3 = 4(0)$ is true. Leave it there --- \emph{why} dividing lost it is problem 11.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} Jordan's algebra had no mistake in it. So where did a solution
    go? Today we solve by factoring, and the degree tells us when we have found them all.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- I Do / We Do / You Do]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{I DO --- Part 1: Factor First, by a Checklist} (10 min)

\textit{Notes rows FACTOR FIRST and FACTORING STRATEGY: read each definition, mark up the
displays, work problems 1 and 3. Teacher works; students watch and annotate. Nothing is
cold-called in this phase.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the Zero Product Property. Point at $p(x) = x(x + 4)(x - 3)$: follow each arrow
        to its factor set equal to zero, fill the three label boxes ($0$, $-4$, $3$), then
        touch the three gold dots on the graph --- factor, zero, intercept (3.3).
  \item Work \textbf{problem 1} aloud: ``Everything is on one side already. GCF first ---
        $2x$, and the $x$ stays.'' $2x(x - 5) = 0$ gives $0$ and $5$.
  \item Read the four \stepnum{1}--\stepnum{4} steps. Walk the pattern table; fill
        \textit{prime} for the sum of squares yourself and say why: no real number squared
        and added to 9 makes 0.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 3} by grouping, naming each step: pair, factor each pair, pull
        out $(x + 2)$, then \emph{step 3} --- $x^2 - 9$ is not finished.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 2: GCF and the Patterns} (6 min)

\textit{Problems 2 and 4--6. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 2}: the GCF is $3x^2$, so $x = 0$ is a double zero --- the first
        multiplicity 2 of the day. \textbf{Problem 4} is GCF, then squares.
  \item \textbf{Problem 5}: name $a = 3x$ and $b = 1$ \emph{before} writing the pattern; the
        middle sign of the trinomial is the opposite of the binomial's.
  \item \textbf{Problem 6}: say ``let $u = x^2$'' out loud, factor $u^2 - 5u + 4$, put $x^2$
        back, then squares twice.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 3: Every Solution, Real or Not} (8 min)

\textit{Notes row EVERY SOLUTION. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.
Teacher works problem 7; the class works the three label boxes and problems 8--10.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Work \textbf{problem 7} against the graph: two crossings, but degree 4. Fill the label
        boxes ($2$, $4$, \textit{conjugate}); the other two are $\pm i$, real zeros of nothing
        on this graph.
  \item \textbf{Problem 8}: $x^2 + 9$ is at least 9, so the graph never reaches the axis.
        \textbf{Problem 9} is grouping again, ending in $\pm 2i$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 10} is the one quadratic-formula case: keep the numbers on the board
        and simplify $\sqrt{-12} = 2i\sqrt{3}$ (warm-up item 3).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 4: Two Paths} (8 min)

\textit{Notes row TWO PATHS --- the crux. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every
one. Teacher works problem 11; the class works 12--14.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Work \textbf{problem 11}: substitute $x = 0$ into $x^3 = 9x$. True --- so Path A lost
        a real solution. Fill the label box with $0$. The count, 3, is the alarm.
  \item \textbf{Problem 12}: dividing by $2x^2$ loses a \emph{double} zero --- two of the four.
        \textbf{Problem 13} is the second trap: finish $x^2 - 4$, and solve $x^2 + 4 = 0$ too.
  \item \textbf{Problem 14} hides the divide: the $x$ is on both sides.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{YOU DO --- Part 5: Factor and Solve} (16 min)

\textit{Notes row FACTOR AND SOLVE, problems 15--18 --- the whole independent phase.
Students work alone; circulate and say nothing a neighbor could say instead.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 15} is GCF and squares. \textbf{16} is the crux transferred ---
        $5x^3 = 20x^2$, where dividing leaves only $x = 4$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 17} is the second trap transferred: $x^4 - 81$ is not done at
        $(x^2 - 9)(x^2 + 9)$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question: GCF $x$, quadratic form, a prime
        $x^2 + 3$ --- five zeros, three on the graph.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: The Factoring Checklist]{lilac}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}
\textbf{Steps}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Move every term to one side; the other side is 0.
  \item Take out the GCF --- the number \emph{and} the power of $x$. Never divide it away.
  \item Count the terms: 2 --- squares or cubes; 3 --- trinomial or quadratic form; 4 ---
        grouping.
  \item Factor completely: test every factor again.
  \item Set each factor equal to zero; use the quadratic formula on a prime quadratic.
  \item Count: the number of solutions, with multiplicity, equals the degree.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.5\linewidth}
\textbf{Worked example} --- solve $2x^4 = 2x$.
\[\begin{aligned}
  2x^4 - 2x &= 0 \\
  2x(x^3 - 1) &= 0 \\
  2x(x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1) &= 0
\end{aligned}\]
$x = 0$, $x = 1$, and $x^2 + x + 1 = 0$ gives
$x = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4}}{2} = -\dfrac{1}{2} \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,i$. Four solutions for
degree 4. Dividing by $2x$ would have left $x^3 = 1$ and lost $x = 0$.
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the \textbf{You Do} phase --- the sixteen minutes where students are working
problems 15--18 without you --- and check. The crux is \textbf{problem 11} (with 12 and 14);
its You Do transfer is \textbf{problem 16}. The second trap is \textbf{problem 13}; its transfer
is \textbf{problem 17}, and \textbf{problem 18} needs both.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Watch for (the target misconception):} a student who divides both sides of
        $5x^3 = 20x^2$ by $5x^2$ and writes only $x = 4$. Ask: ``Is $x = 0$ a solution? Check
        it. How many solutions should a degree-3 equation have?''
  \item \textbf{Watch for (the second trap):} $x^4 - 81 = (x^2 - 9)(x^2 + 9)$ and the answer
        $\pm 3$. Ask: ``Is every factor prime? Did you set \emph{every} factor equal to zero?
        Count them against the degree.''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} the sum-of-cubes trinomial with the wrong middle sign,
        $9x^2 + 3x + 1$. Point at the pattern table: the binomial's sign, then the opposite.
  \item \textbf{Common error:} quadratic form left in $u$ --- ``$u = 4$ or $u = -1$'' as the
        answer. Ask: ``What is $u$? Put $x^2$ back.''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} $x^2 + 9 = 0$ answered ``no solution.'' Ask: ``No \emph{real}
        solution --- and how many does a degree-2 equation have?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Differentiation --- During You Do (16 min)]{redbox}
There is no separate activity sheet: students work the notes' You Do row (problems 15--18)
alone while you circulate. Differentiate by \textit{where you stand}, not by handing out
different paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Support.} Sit with a struggling student on \textbf{problem 15} and run the
        checklist out loud with them: ``Step 1, GCF --- what do all three terms share?'' Have
        them write the step number in the margin beside each line. The habit carries 16 and 17.
  \item \textbf{On level.} \textbf{Problem 16} is the crux transfer. Do not answer it; if you
        see $x = 4$ alone, ask ``degree?'' and ``try $x = 0$,'' and walk away.
        \textbf{Problem 17} should clear unaided once a student counts four solutions.
  \item \textbf{Extend.} \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question: GCF $x$, quadratic
        form, squares, and a prime $x^2 + 3$ --- five zeros, of which the graph shows three.
        A finisher who explains that the other two are a conjugate pair, not intercepts the
        graph forgot, presents in the Debrief.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (5 min)]{redbox}
Whole class, teacher-led, packets still open. Three moves, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Share out the You Do} (3 min) --- one answer per problem, not a full review.
        Problem 15: $0, \pm 2$. Problem 16: $0$ twice and $4$ --- ``three, counting
        multiplicity,'' said aloud. Problem 17: $\pm 3$ and $\pm 3i$. Problem 18: $0, \pm 2,
        \pm i\sqrt{3}$. If a student wrote 18 cleanly, they present it --- it is the only
        time the class hears both traps and the degree count in one problem.
  \item \textbf{Name the headline} (1 min) --- say it, then make the class say it back:
        \textit{move everything to one side and factor --- never divide by $x$. Factor
        completely, set every factor to zero, and count: degree $n$ means $n$ solutions.}
        Point at the two paths in the TWO PATHS row while you say it.
  \item \textbf{Point forward} (1 min) --- the DeltaMath assignment \textit{Lesson 3.4} and its
        due date: write the date on the board and have students copy it into cover row 4.
        Then: 3.5 handles the polynomial no pattern fits, such as $x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$ ---
        division finds its factors.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Do not} re-teach the cubes patterns here or take new questions. If the You Do showed
they did not land, it is the 3.5 warm-up's job, not this minute's.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 2 matters more than it looks: (a) and (b) take
out a GCF that \emph{includes a power of $x$}, which is exactly the factor today's crux is
about --- a student who writes $4x^3 - 10x^2 = x^2(4x - 10)$ has not taken the whole GCF, and one
who writes $2(2x^3 - 5x^2)$ has left the $x^2$ behind. Item 2(b), $3x^2(x^2 + 4)$, quietly
plants a sum of squares that will not factor. Item 3 is the $i$ arithmetic problem 10 needs.
Item 4 is 3.3 read forward --- zeros, multiplicity, touch or cross, degree equals the count ---
and today runs it in reverse, so read its (c) aloud during the hook.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The notes are one Main Ideas / Notes table, four instruction rows and a You Do row; in each
instruction row you read the printed sentences, mark up the display, and work the first problem
of the grid (1, 3, 7, 11), and the class works the rest with the pen in their hand, every problem
cold-called. Say the checklist step out loud every time you factor --- ``step 1, GCF; step 2,
four terms, grouping; step 3, is $x^2 - 9$ finished?'' --- because that narration is what a
student repeats alone in the You Do.

EVERY SOLUTION is where the count first matters: the graph shows two crossings and the degree
says four. Do not let ``no solution'' stand for $x^2 + 9 = 0$; it is ``no real solution,'' and
the conjugate pair is the rest of the count. Problem 10 is the only quadratic-formula problem
in the notes on purpose --- keep its numbers small and on the board.

TWO PATHS is the lesson. The two worked paths are printed side by side so the disagreement is
visible before anyone argues: three solutions against two. Problem 11's substitution, $0^3 =
9(0)$, settles it in one line --- make a student say ``dividing by $x$ assumes $x$ is not zero.''
Problem 13 is the second trap and pairs it with the count: degree 4, so four solutions. The You
Do (15--18) is the entire independent phase and carries sixteen minutes; problem 16 is the crux
in a new equation, 17 the second trap, 18 both plus a graph. If the clock slips, cut problems 5
and 9 and let DeltaMath carry them.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Debrief]
Five minutes, and on this lesson they are at risk --- five strategies share the guided phase and
it will try to borrow them. Borrow from problem 5 (sum of cubes; problem 10 and the DeltaMath
cubes topic cover the pattern) or problem 9 (grouping again) instead. Never borrow from the You
Do: with no exit ticket, circulating it is the last look you get before the work leaves the room.
The share-out is one answer per You Do problem, said with its count --- ``three, counting
multiplicity'' --- so a student who stalled on 16 still hears that $x = 0$ belongs in the answer,
which the DeltaMath diagnostic will otherwise mark wrong at home without telling them why.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
Extra credit, optional, scored 1 to 5, and not a substitute for the DeltaMath assignment --- say
so when you hand out the packet. Its 1-to-5 score is added as extra-credit points.
Multiple-choice answers are \textbf{C, B, A, D, C}. Item 1 is the most useful one to glance at:
(A) $\pm 4$ only is the target misconception exactly --- divided by $x$ --- and (D) $4$ only
divided and took the positive root; (B) kept 0 but never took the square root. Item 2: (A)
stopped early; (C) and (D) misread $x^2 + 1$. Item 3: (B) and (D) are the sign errors in the
cubes pattern; (C) treats $a^3 + b^3$ as $(a + b)^3$. Item 4: (A) dropped the factor $x^2 + 2$
as ``no solution''; (B) lost the $i$; (C) solved for $u = x^2$ and stopped. Item 5:
(A) divided by $2x^2$; (B) found 0 but not its multiplicity; (D) stopped at $x^2 = 9$. In the
free response, (a) needs a context reason for each of $0$, $4$, $5$; (b) earns full credit only
with the degree argument (three zeros); in (c) dividing both sides by 4 is fine --- dividing by a
nonzero \emph{number} loses nothing --- and the point is rejecting $x = 6$ because the cut must be
less than 4 inches.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
DeltaMath, graded, due on the date you set (assignment \textit{Lesson 3.4}). The diagnostic is
\textbf{Mixed Multistep Factoring (Equation)}: its equations take out a GCF with a power of $x$
before a pattern, so a student who divides by $x$ submits a solution set missing $x = 0$ and is
marked wrong --- a miss there is almost always the target misconception. If it is common, open
3.5 with a warm-up item that puts the two paths for $x^3 = 25x$ side by side. A miss on
\textbf{Solving Quartic Equations} that lists only the real solutions is the second trap
(stopping early, or ``no solution'' for $x^2 + k = 0$). DeltaMath cannot ask a student to
explain \emph{why} dividing by $x$ loses a solution, so read \textbf{You Do problem 16} in the
packets as well. \textbf{Factoring Diff / Sum of Cubes (Level 1)} and \textbf{Finding
x-Intercepts in Unfactored Form} predict 3.5: a student who factors a cubic into a linear
factor times a quadratic, and solves the quadratic, is ready to get that same linear-times-quadratic
