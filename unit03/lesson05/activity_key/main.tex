\begin{scenariobox}[Sinusoidal Functions in the Real World]{sky}
Sinusoidal functions appear everywhere in nature and technology: sound waves,
tides, seasonal temperature patterns, alternating electric current, and
the motion of a pendulum.  In each case, the key characteristics ---
amplitude, midline, period, and frequency --- tell us something meaningful
about the phenomenon.
\end{scenariobox}

\vspace{0.1in}

%=======================================================================
% TIER R
%=======================================================================
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40,
    title=\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate --- Key},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=2mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm]

Use the formulas:
\[
  \text{Amplitude} = \frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}
  \qquad
  \text{Midline: } y = \frac{\text{max} + \text{min}}{2}
  \qquad
  \text{Frequency} = \frac{1}{\text{period}}
\]

\textbf{Function 1.} Max $= 4$, min $= -4$, one cycle from $0$ to $2\pi$.

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
  Amplitude $=$ \ans{4} &
  Midline $=$ \ans{$y = 0$} &
  Period $=$ \ans{$2\pi$} &
  Frequency $=$ \ans{$1/(2\pi)$}
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.15in}

\textbf{Function 2.} Max $= 3$, min $= -1$.

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
  Amplitude $= \ans{2}$ &
  Midline $= \ans{$y = 1$}$
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.1in}

Is the midline $y = 0$ here?  Why or why not?

\medskip
\ans{No. The midline is $y = 1$ because the average of $3$ and $-1$ is $1$, not
$0$. The midline is only $y = 0$ when the max and min are opposite in sign and
equal in magnitude.}

\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{Function P:}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$\theta$ & $0$ & $\pi/2$ & $\pi$ & $3\pi/2$ & $2\pi$ & $5\pi/2$ & $3\pi$ \\

$P(\theta)$ & $3$ & $5$ & $3$ & $1$ & $3$ & $5$ & $3$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\textbf{Function Q:}

\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$\theta$ & $0$ & $\pi/4$ & $\pi/2$ & $3\pi/4$ & $\pi$ & $5\pi/4$ & $3\pi/2$ \\
\hline
$Q(\theta)$ & $0$ & $2$ & $0$ & $-2$ & $0$ & $2$ & $0$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
  & \textbf{Amplitude} & \textbf{Midline} & \textbf{Period} & \textbf{Frequency} \\[4pt]
  Function $P$: & \ans{2} & \ans{$y=3$} & \ans{$2\pi$} & \ans{$1/(2\pi)$} \\[8pt]
  Function $Q$: & \ans{2} & \ans{$y=0$} & \ans{$\pi$}  & \ans{$1/\pi$}
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.12in}

Function $P$ is \ans{neither} because:

\ans{For $P$ to be even we would need $P(-\theta) = P(\theta)$, and for odd
$P(-\theta) = -P(\theta)$.  Checking $\theta = \pi/2$: $P(\pi/2) = 5$ but
$P(-\pi/2)$ is not listed in the table; however, $P$ has midline $y = 3 \ne 0$,
so it cannot be odd.  And the values are not symmetric about the $y$-axis in the
standard sense because the function is shifted vertically.}

Function $Q$ is \ans{odd} because:

\ans{$Q(-\theta) = -Q(\theta)$.  For example, $Q(\pi/4)=2$ and $Q(-\pi/4)=-2$.
The midline is $y=0$ and the values are negated when $\theta$ is negated, which
matches the definition of an odd function.}

\end{tcolorbox}

    title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extend --- Key},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=2mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm]

\textbf{Part 1.} Amplitude $= 2$, midline $y = 1$, period $= \pi$.

(a) Maximum and minimum values:

\medskip
\ans{max $= $ midline $+$ amplitude $= 1 + 2 = 3$}

\ans{min $= $ midline $-$ amplitude $= 1 - 2 = -1$}

\medskip
(b) Frequency:

\medskip
\ans{Frequency $= \dfrac{1}{\pi}$}

\vspace{0.1in}

\textbf{Part 2.}

\ans{On the unit circle, a point at angle $\theta$ has coordinates
$(\cos\theta,\, \sin\theta)$.  The point at angle $\pi - \theta$ is the
reflection of the point at $\theta$ across the $y$-axis.  Reflecting across the
$y$-axis changes the sign of the $x$-coordinate but leaves the $y$-coordinate
unchanged.  Therefore the $y$-coordinate at $\pi - \theta$ equals the
$y$-coordinate at $\theta$, which means $\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta$.}

\begin{teachernote}
Full-credit responses should (1) invoke the unit circle interpretation of sine as
the $y$-coordinate, and (2) explain why the $y$-coordinates of $\theta$ and
$\pi-\theta$ are equal (reflection over the $y$-axis).  Algebraic approaches
using the sum formula are acceptable for extra credit discussion.
\end{teachernote}
