\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item I can divide one polynomial by another with long division and write the result as
        dividend $=$ divisor $\cdot$ quotient $+$ remainder, with the remainder lower in degree
        than the divisor.
  \item I can divide a polynomial by $x - c$ with synthetic division, writing a $0$ for every
        missing power and choosing the right sign for $c$.
  \item I can use the Remainder Theorem: the remainder when $p(x)$ is divided by $x - c$ is
        $p(c)$.
  \item I can use the Factor Theorem --- $(x - c)$ is a factor exactly when $p(c) = 0$ --- to
        divide out one known zero and find all the zeros of a cubic.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small
Dividing polynomials works the way dividing numbers does: $157 = 12 \cdot 13 + 1$, and
$x^2 + 5x + 7 = (x + 2)(x + 3) + 1$. When the divisor is $x - c$, the remainder is no
accident --- it is the value $p(c)$. So a remainder of \textbf{zero} says three things at once:
$x - c$ is a \textbf{factor}, $c$ is a \textbf{zero}, and $(c, 0)$ is an \textbf{$x$-intercept}.
Every power keeps its own column, even the ones that are missing.
\end{remindbox}
