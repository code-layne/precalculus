\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Skills} &
\textbf{Key Understandings} \\
\midrule
\textbf{AP Skill 2.A} --- Identify information from graphical, numerical, analytical, and
verbal representations to answer a question or construct a model. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item A polynomial function of degree $n$ can be written
        $p(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$ with $a_n \ne 0$; $a_n x^n$
        is the \textbf{leading term}, $a_n$ the \textbf{leading coefficient}, $a_0$ the
        constant term, and a nonzero constant has degree 0. (1.4.A.1)
  \item In factored form the degree is the \textbf{sum} of the factors' exponents and the
        leading coefficient is the \textbf{product} of their leading coefficients --- found
        without expanding.
  \item Read backwards from a graph: $k$ turning points means degree at least $k + 1$;
        $m$ $x$-intercepts means degree at least $m$; matching arms means even degree.
\end{itemize} \\
\textbf{AP Skill 3.A} --- Describe the characteristics of a function with varying levels of
precision, depending on the function's representation. &
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item As $x \to \pm\infty$ the leading term outgrows every other term, so it alone sets the
        end behavior, written $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} p(x) = \pm\infty$. (1.6.A.1--1.6.A.3)
  \item Even degree: the arms match, so the function has an absolute minimum (arms up) or an
        absolute maximum (arms down). Odd degree: the arms oppose --- neither. (1.4.A.4)
  \item A degree-$n$ polynomial has \textbf{at most} $n - 1$ turning points and \textbf{at
        most} $n$ real zeros. These are ceilings, not counts: $y = x^3$ has no turning points
        and $y = x^2 + 1$ has no real zeros. (Course addition --- not in the CED.)
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} \emph{the leading term is the first term written.}
        In $p(x) = 5 + 3x - 2x^4$ the leading term is $-2x^4$, not 5; in
        $q(x) = -(x-1)^2(x+3)(2x+1)$ it is $-2x^4$, from $2 + 1 + 1$ and $(-1)(1)(1)(2)$.
        The wrong leading term gives the wrong end behavior.
\end{itemize} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\leavevmode\bfseries\color{plum}}p{0.24\linewidth} X @{}}
Polynomial function & $p(x) = a_n x^n + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$, with $n$ a whole number, real coefficients, and $a_n \ne 0$. \\
Standard form       & The terms written from the highest power of $x$ down to the constant term. \\
Degree              & The highest power of $x$, $n$; a nonzero constant has degree 0. \\
Leading term        & The term with the highest power, $a_n x^n$ --- wherever it is written. \\
Leading coefficient & The number $a_n$ multiplying the leading term, sign included. \\
Constant term       & The term with no $x$, $a_0$; it is $p(0)$, the $y$-intercept. \\
End behavior        & What $p(x)$ does as $x \to \infty$ and $x \to -\infty$, written in limit notation (from 2.4). \\
Turning point       & A point where the graph switches between increasing and decreasing --- a relative extremum (from 3.1). \\
Real zero           & An input $a$ with $p(a) = 0$: an $x$-intercept of the graph. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review (5 min)]{lilac}
    Please see attached warm-up (spiral review).
    Skills reviewed: the sign of a power of a negative number --- even powers positive, odd
    powers negative, the arithmetic every end-behavior case rests on; multiplying two binomials
    and naming the highest-power term of the product (factored form, row 4);
    writing end behavior in limit notation from a graph (Lesson 2.4);
    and naming relative and absolute extrema on a polynomial graph whose arms both rise
    (Lesson 3.1) --- a quiet preview of ``even degree means an absolute extremum.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook (3 min)]{lilac}
    \textbf{A race between two functions.} On the board: $f(x) = x^4$ and $g(x) = 500x^3$.
    \begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
        \item Ask who is ahead at $x = 10$. Take a vote, then compute: $f(10) = 10{,}000$,
              $g(10) = 500{,}000$. The 500 looks unbeatable.
        \item Ask again at $x = 1000$: $f = 10^{12}$, $g = 5 \times 10^{11}$. The lead has
              flipped --- and it never flips back, because each step multiplies $x^4$ by one
              more factor of $x$ than $x^3$.
    \end{enumerate}
    \textit{Discussion prompt:} so what decides who wins in the long run --- the coefficient or
    the exponent? Land on: \emph{far from zero, the highest power wins.} That term gets a name
    today --- the leading term --- and it decides where both ends of the graph go.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- I Do / We Do / You Do]{lilac}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{I DO --- Part 1: Anatomy and the Four Cases} (10 min)

\textit{Notes rows ANATOMY and END BEHAVIOR: read each definition, mark up each display, work
problems 1 and 3. Teacher works; students watch and annotate. Nothing is cold-called in this
phase.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the ANATOMY sentences, then fill the four label boxes on
        $p(x) = -3x^4 + 2x^3 - x + 7$ yourself: \textit{leading coefficient} on the $-3$
        (``sign included''), \textit{degree} on the 4, \textit{leading term} under $-3x^4$,
        \textit{constant term} on the 7. Fill the vocabulary box as each term is named.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 1}: $f(x) = 4x^3 - x^2 + 6$ --- four answers, said as a sentence.
  \item Read the END BEHAVIOR sentences and connect them to the hook: the leading term wins
        far from 0. Fill the four-case display in limit notation, one graph at a time, and
        say each in words: ``even and positive --- both arms up.'' Read the lilac box on
        absolute extrema aloud.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 3}, $p(x) = 2x^4 - 5x^3 + x$: name the leading term
        \emph{before} anything else, then both limits.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 2: Naming and Reading the Ends} (6 min)

\textit{Problems 2 and 4--6. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 2}: $g$ has constant term 0 (there is none written) and $h(x) = 9$ has
        degree 0 --- both surprise someone.
  \item \textbf{Problems 4 and 5}: odd and negative; then $-0.5x^6 + 100x^5$, where the big
        coefficient tempts students away from the bigger exponent. Tie 5 back to the hook.
  \item \textbf{Problem 6} reverses the reading: from the arms to the degree and the sign.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 3: At Most, Not Exactly} (7 min)

\textit{Notes row COUNTING. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call every one. The teacher
works problem 7; the class works 8--10.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Read the two ceilings, then fill the table for graph A yourself, counting turning points
        and intercepts with a finger on the graph. Cold-call B and C.
  \item \textbf{Problem 7} (teacher): degree 5 --- at most 4 and at most 5.
        \textbf{Problem 8} reads backwards: least degree from the counts.
  \item \textbf{Problems 9 and 10} are the secondary trap: B has degree 4 and \emph{no}
        intercepts; C is a cubic with \emph{no} turning points. Make the class say ``at most''
        out loud before anyone writes.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{WE DO --- Part 4: Which Term Leads?} (8 min)

\textit{Notes row WHICH TERM LEADS? --- the crux. Class fills the blanks together; cold-call
every one. Teacher works problem 11; the class works 12--14.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fill the display: for $p$, \textit{written first} is 5 and the \textit{leading term}
        is $-2x^4$. For $q$, write the leading piece of each factor in its box ($-1$, $x^2$,
        $x$, $2x$) and multiply: $-2x^4$. Nobody expands.
  \item Work \textbf{problem 11} on $p$. \textbf{Problem 12} is the misconception in a
        student's mouth: Jordan read $3x^2$ because it came first.
  \item \textbf{Problem 13 is the crux}: $q$'s degree from $2 + 1 + 1$ and its leading
        coefficient from $(-1)(1)(1)(2)$. Ask what a student who used the first factor's
        sign alone would say. \textbf{Problem 14} hides the negative inside $(4 - x)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{YOU DO --- Part 5: Read It All} (16 min)

\textit{Notes row READ IT ALL, problems 15--18 --- the whole independent phase. Students work
alone; circulate and say nothing a neighbor could say instead.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem 15} is the crux transferred to scrambled order, with both ceilings
        added; \textbf{16} transfers it to factored form, with a cube to raise:
        $(2x)^3 = 8x^3$, not $2x^3$.
  \item \textbf{Problem 17} reads graph D: arms, sign, least degree --- and why 5 is
        impossible but 6 is allowed (the ceiling trap again).
  \item \textbf{Problem 18} is the finisher's question: every cubic crosses the $x$-axis.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the \textbf{You Do} phase --- the sixteen minutes where students are working
problems 15--18 without you --- and check. The crux is \textbf{problem 13} ($q$ in factored
form: degree 4, leading coefficient $-2$, both arms down); its You Do transfers are
\textbf{problem 15} (out of order) and \textbf{problem 16} (factored, with a cube).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Common error (the crux):} reading the first term written as the leading term
        --- in 15, $6x$ gives ``odd, positive,'' the opposite of the truth. Point at the
        exponents: ``Which power is biggest? Where is it?''
  \item \textbf{Common error (the crux, factored):} in 16, taking the first factor's 2 or
        forgetting the cube, so the leading coefficient comes out $2$ or $-2$ instead of $-8$;
        or dropping the negative hidden in $(5 - x)$. Ask: ``What is the leading piece of
        $(5 - x)$? Of $(2x - 1)^3$?''
  \item \textbf{Common error:} counting factors instead of adding exponents --- calling $q$
        degree 3. Cold-call: ``How many $x$'s does $(x - 1)^2$ hand you?''
  \item \textbf{Common error (secondary trap):} reading $n - 1$ and $n$ as exact counts ---
        in 17, ``it can't be degree 6, that would need 5 turning points.'' Ask: ``Is 5 the
        count or the most?''
  \item \textbf{Limit notation:} writing $\lim_{x \to \infty}$ for both ends, or putting the
        arm's direction under the lim. ``Under lim is the input; after the equals sign is the
        output'' --- the 2.4 sentence.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Differentiation --- During You Do (16 min)]{redbox}
There is no separate activity sheet: students work the notes' You Do row (problems 15--18)
alone while you circulate. Differentiate by \textit{where you stand}, not by handing out
different paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Support.} Sit with a struggling student on \textbf{problem 15} and have them
        circle the highest exponent before writing anything --- then rewrite $f$ in standard
        form above the problem. Once $-x^3$ is first on the page, the four-case display does
        the rest.
  \item \textbf{On level.} \textbf{Problem 16} is the factored transfer. Do not answer it;
        have them write the leading piece of each factor under it, as in the row-4 display,
        and multiply. Walk away once $(2x)^3$ is on the page.
  \item \textbf{Extend.} \textbf{Problems 17 and 18} are the finisher's questions. 17 asks
        why an odd degree is impossible for graph D but degree 6 is fine --- the parity of the
        arms against the ceiling of turning points. 18 asks them to \emph{prove} a cubic has a
        real zero from its end behavior alone; a student who says ``one arm is below the
        axis and the other above, and the graph has no gaps'' has the whole idea. Either
        answer presents in the Debrief.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard[20]
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (5 min)]{redbox}
Whole class, teacher-led, packets still open. Three moves, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Share out the You Do} (3 min) --- one answer per problem, not a full review.
        Problem 15: the leading term $-x^3$, and the limits. Problem 16: the leading
        coefficient $-8$, with the cube shown. Problem 17: least degree 4, and why not 5.
        Problem 18: no --- the arms force a crossing. If a student wrote 18 cleanly, they
        present it; DeltaMath will not ask it.
  \item \textbf{Name the headline} (1 min) --- say it, then make the class say it back:
        \textit{the leading term is the highest power, wherever it is written --- its degree
        and its sign decide both ends. The degree is a ceiling: at most $n - 1$ turns and at
        most $n$ zeros.}
  \item \textbf{Point forward} (1 min) --- the DeltaMath assignment, \textit{Lesson 3.2}, and
        its due date: write it on the board and have students copy it into row 4 of the
        cover. This is the last lesson of the semester; in January, 3.3 opens with the
        zeros: where the graph crosses, why it sometimes only touches, and why a degree-$n$
        polynomial has exactly $n$ zeros once complex numbers are counted.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Do not} re-teach end behavior here or take new questions. If the share-out shows the
leading term did not land, it is the first warm-up after the break that carries it, not this
minute.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- DeltaMath, graded.} Assign as \textit{Lesson 3.2}, in this order:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Polynomial Terminology} (4 problems) --- matches notes problems 1--2 and the
        degree and leading coefficient of 11, 13.
  \item \textbf{End Behavior Algebraically} (5 problems) \textit{(diagnostic)} --- matches
        notes problems 3--5 and 11--16.
  \item \textbf{Justify End Behavior (Limit Notation)} (3 problems) --- matches the limit
        notation in problems 3--5 and 11.
  \item \textbf{End Behavior Graphically} (3 problems) --- matches the four-case display and
        problem 6.
  \item \textbf{Even and Odd Degree from a Graph} (3 problems) --- matches problems 6 and 17.
  \item \textbf{Determine Features of Polynomial Graph} (3 problems) --- matches the
        COUNTING row, problems 7--10, and problem 17.
\end{enumerate}
DeltaMath mostly gives polynomials in standard order, so it rarely tests the crux --- a
leading term that is not written first, or one hidden in factored form. That rides on You Do
problems 15 and 16 (and AP Practice items 1, 2, 5, and 6(c)).
\textit{Leave out:} Determine Number of X-Intercepts, Finding Roots From a Graph, Find
Multiplicity of Zeroes (Polynomials), and Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (actual zeros are
3.3's job, after the break); Odd and Even Polynomial Functions (symmetry, $f(-x)$, from 2.3
--- not even and odd \emph{degree}); Relative vs.\ Absolute Extrema and Successive Differences
(Polynomial) (3.1); Write Polynomial Function From Graph (needs the zeros, 3.3--3.4).

\textbf{AP Practice --- extra credit, scored 1 to 5 (2 pages).} Optional, last in the packet.
Section I is five AP-style multiple-choice items (four options) whose distractors are the
lesson's errors: the first terms written read as the leading term (1), factors counted instead
of exponents added (2), a graph matched with the wrong sign, the wrong parity, or a degree too
low for its turning points (3), the ceilings $n - 1$ and $n$ read as exact counts (4), and one
hidden negative counted but not the other (5). Section II is one free-response question on a
pre-drawn degree-5 graph: end behavior in limit notation and whether an absolute maximum exists
(a), the least possible degree from turning points, intercepts, and arms (b), and whether a
proposed factored formula with two hidden negatives could be the function (c) --- the
finisher's question, the crux in reverse.

\textbf{Preview:} Next lesson (3.3, Zeros of Polynomial Functions --- January) turns the
ceiling of $n$ real zeros into a count: factored form shows each zero, its multiplicity
decides whether the graph crosses or touches, and complex zeros come in conjugate pairs ---
so a degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ zeros, real and complex together.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Five minutes, silent start, no calculators. Item 1 is the arithmetic under every end-behavior
case: a student who writes $(-10)^3 = 1000$ will put the left arm of every odd polynomial on
the wrong side of the axis. Item 2's ``term with the highest power'' is $3x^2$ --- the product
of the two leading terms, $x \cdot 3x$; say that out loud in passing, because row 4 runs on
it. Item 3 is 2.4's limit notation on a graph that is not a polynomial; a student who writes
$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \infty$ because ``$x$ goes to infinity'' is reading the input as
the output --- flag them before the END BEHAVIOR row. Item 4(b) asks for an absolute minimum
and \emph{no} absolute maximum on a graph whose arms both rise --- the even-degree fact the
notes will name, discovered here first.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The notes are one Main Ideas / Notes table, four instruction rows and a You Do row; in each
instruction row you read the printed definition, mark up the display, and work the first
problem of the grid (1, 3, 7, 11), and the class works the rest with the pen in their hand,
every problem cold-called. ANATOMY and END BEHAVIOR share the I Do: they are one idea --- the
leading term --- named and then used. On the four-case display, say each case in words before
writing the limits, and make ``a positive leading coefficient sends the \emph{right} arm up''
the sentence students repeat; it is the fastest check there is.

COUNTING comes before the crux on purpose: it is calmer, and its secondary trap (problems 9
and 10) teaches ``at most'' while students still trust the formulas. Count turning points with
a finger moving left to right --- every change from rising to falling or back.

WHICH TERM LEADS? is the lesson. Students saw one out-of-order polynomial in 2.4 (notes
problem 14 there); today the order is scrambled harder and the factored form is new. Do the
display before any problem: the boxes under $q$'s factors \emph{are} the method. Do not expand
$q$, ever --- the point is that nobody has to. Problem 13 asks for the leading coefficient
\emph{and} the absolute maximum; a student who gets $+2$ will say ``absolute minimum,'' and
that pair of wrong answers is exactly what the AP exam rewards checking.

The You Do row (15--18) is the entire independent phase and carries sixteen minutes. 15 and
16 are the crux transferred; 17 is the ceiling trap with a parity twist; 18 is a finisher's
proof. If the clock slips, cut problem 18 and let the Debrief carry it.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Debrief]
Five minutes, and on this lesson they are at risk --- the guided phase runs 21 minutes and the
day before a break is never quiet. Borrow from problem 18 instead, never from the You Do's first
three problems: 18 is a finisher's question, and the Debrief can show one student's answer to
everyone. The point-forward minute matters more than usual: the DeltaMath due date may fall
over the break, so say it aloud and write it down, and tell students plainly that January
starts with zeros, so this ceiling of $n$ will become a count. The share-out is one answer per
You Do problem so a student who stalled on 15 still hears $(2x)^3 = 8x^3$ in 16 --- the exact
slip the diagnostic DeltaMath topic will not catch.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
Extra credit, optional, scored 1 to 5, and not a substitute for the DeltaMath assignment ---
say so when you hand out the packet. Its 1-to-5 score is added as extra-credit points.
Multiple-choice answers are \textbf{A, B, C, D, B}. Item 1 is the one to glance at: (B) means
the student read $4 + 7x^2$ --- the first terms written --- as the leading term (the lesson's
misconception), (C) means the right sign but an even reading of an odd degree, (D) means the
sign was dropped. In item 2, (A) counts factors instead of adding exponents, (D) multiplies
the exponents, (C) loses the $-2$. In item 3, (A) has the wrong sign, (B) the wrong parity,
and (D) a degree too low for three turning points. Item 4's (A) and (B) are the ceilings read
as exact counts; (C) gives an odd degree an absolute maximum. Item 5's (A) counts one of the
two negatives in $(20 - 2x)(12 - 2x)$ and not the other. In the free response, accept the
limits in (a) in either order; (b) needs at least two of the three reasons for full credit;
(c) is the deepest question on the page --- credit any answer that finds the formula's leading
term $+x^5$ and says its arms oppose the graph's, whatever words it uses for ``arms.''
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
DeltaMath, graded, assigned as \textit{Lesson 3.2} and due on the date you set --- likely over
or just after the break, so say which. \textbf{End Behavior Algebraically} is the diagnostic:
check its item-level results when students return. A miss with the right parity and the wrong
sign usually means the leading coefficient's sign was read from the wrong term; a miss with the
wrong parity means the degree was read from the first term. Either way it is the crux, so
re-teach it from the row-4 display, not from the four-case table. DeltaMath gives
mostly standard-order polynomials and cannot ask \emph{why} a cubic must have a real zero:
the scrambled and factored forms live in You Do problems 15 and 16 and the proof in 18, so
read those on the packet when you collect it. \textbf{Determine Features of Polynomial Graph}
and the degree reading in \textbf{Polynomial Terminology} predict 3.3, where factored form is
read for its zeros and their multiplicities; if either is weak, put a factored leading-term
item like problem 16 in the first warm-up of January.
\end{teachernote}
